The lawspeaker, or *lagman*, was an office that the entry calls unique to Scandinavia. This piece uses one source, an entry of an encyclopaedia in English, and notes where the entry itself warns that it needs more citations.

## What the office was

The entry says that the office has its basis in a common Germanic oral tradition, in which wise people recited the law, but only in Scandinavia did this become an office. At first, lawspeakers represented the people, and their duties and authority were tied to the assemblies, the *things*. For most of the last thousand years, they were part of the king’s administration. The names vary: *lagman* in Swedish, *lovsigemand* in Danish, *lagmann* in Norwegian, *lögsögumaður* in Icelandic, *laamanni* in Finnish.

## In Iceland

In Iceland, the office was introduced in 930, when the Althing was established. The traditional date is 930, and Úlfljótr appears as a founding figure and original author of the laws. The lawspeaker was elected for three years and was to recite the law at the Althing, a third each summer. Besides presiding over the assembly, his duties were limited to counselling and reciting. It was the only government office of the medieval Icelandic Commonwealth. Apart from reciting the law and chairing the court, he had no formal power and was often appointed arbitrator in disputes.

The entry lists the lawspeakers. After Úlfljótr come Hrafn Hængsson, from 930 to 949, Þórarinn Ragabróðir, from 950 to 969, Þorkell máni, from 970 to 984, Þorgeir Ljósvetningagoði, from 985 to 1001, Grímr Svertingsson, from 1002 to 1003, and Skafti Þóroddsson, from 1004 to 1030. Grímr’s term was cut short not for incompetence or illness, but because his voice was too weak. Snorri Sturluson, a famous lawspeaker, held the office from 1215 to 1218 and from 1222 to 1231. After the union with Norway, in 1264, two royal lawspeakers were appointed. The office was abolished with the Althing in 1800.

## In Sweden

In Sweden, the office was the most important one in regional government. Each *lagsaga*, usually a province, was the jurisdiction of a lawspeaker, subordinate to the one of Tiundaland. He presided over the *things*, acted as judge, formulated laws decided by the people and had to memorize the law and recite it. He also recognized the elected king, on behalf of the people, when the king passed on the Eriksgata. Under the Västgöta Law, the yeomen of the province chose the lawspeaker for life from among themselves. The office was not hereditary, but usually came from more powerful families. From the mid-13th century, lawspeakers grew more attached to the king. The office was abolished in 1849, and the title of *lagman* returned in 1947 for senior judges.

## In Norway and Finland

In Norway, lawspeakers were counsellors versed in the law until King Sverre I, who made them officials. In the laws of Magnus VI, they could act as judges and preside at the *lagtings*. Historians see them, before about 1600, as part of the nobility, and there were 10 to 12 in the whole kingdom. The office was abolished in 1797, and the title returned in 1887. Finland was governed by Swedish law until 1809, and the title of *laamanni* returned in 1993 for the chief judge of a district court or a senior judge of appeal.

## In brief

The lawspeaker began tied to the assemblies of the people and became, over the centuries, an office of the king. In Iceland, it lasted three years and recited a third of the law each summer. In Sweden and Norway, he became a judge.

## Terms

*Lagman* is the lawspeaker, in Swedish. *Thing* is the assembly of the people. *Lagsaga* is the area where a regional law applies. *Arbitrator* is someone who decides a dispute that the parties hand to him.

## What remains uncertain

The entry carries at the top a notice that it needs more citations. Historians doubt Snorri’s account of an 11th-century lawspeaker named Torgny. The date of about 1000 for Lum, the first named Swedish lawspeaker, holds only "if the text is correct". The first two successors of Úlfljótr have exactly 20 summers each, which scholars find suspicious, and the Icelandic chronology is probably correct from Þorkell máni onward. The number of lawspeakers in Norway is not given precisely.
