Joik, also written yoik, is a traditional form of song of the Sámi, a people of northern Europe, in Sápmi. This piece uses two sources, an entry of an encyclopaedia in English and a text of the site Nordics.info. Both point out flaws or gaps, and they are marked.

## What it is

According to the entry, the performer is called a *joiker* in Norwegian and in English and a *jojkare* in Swedish. The term joik originally named only one of several Sámi singing styles, but in English it is often used for all traditional Sámi singing. The Nordics.info text says it is a Sámi musical form that resembles singing and has melody and rhythm as its core, sometimes with words, gestures or facial expressions.

## The names

In the Sámi languages, joik is also called *luohti*, *vuolle*, *vuelie* or *juoiggus*. *Luohti* is the North Sámi variant and, according to the entry, the most developed style today. *Vuelie* is the South Sámi style, which retains an old character because its development stopped under Christianity. *Leu'dd*, of the Skolt people, is not a variant of joik, but a different kind of singing. Nordics.info says that only luohti has not mostly died out.

## To joik someone

The Sámi verb, in North Sámi *juoigat*, is transitive. The entry interprets that one joiks a person, rather than "about" them. Nordics.info says the same in another way: a joik is not simply a song about its subject, it evokes it, so that listeners feel it as present, whether a distant loved one or a threatening wolf. The Sámi yoiked other Sámi, animals or nature, and a person’s joik continued to refer to them after death.

## Functions

In small hunting and herding communities, a joik was an identity marker: a young person who took on a role in the community received a joik that stayed with them for life. According to Nordics.info, joiks calmed reindeer and kept predators away, and, in the older worldview, joik and drum helped the *noaidi*, the shaman, to reach ecstasy and travel to other worlds. The text cites the account of Kristoffer Sjulsson, of a girl who sang evening after evening a single word, "vjelha", brother, and her song expressed longing.

## Condemnation and loss

For the entry, during the Christianization of the Sámi, joik was condemned as sinful. It was controversial partly because of its link with the noaidi and with pre-Christian ritual, and because it resembled spells. Nordics.info says that Lutheran missionaries attacked shamanism in the 18th and 19th centuries and banned yoiking, and that, from about 1850 until relatively recently, assimilation pressure made it decline almost to disappearance. Both texts say that, even so, joik remained in the culture.

## The return

Nordics.info credits Nils-Aslak Valkeapää with reviving joik from 1968: he recorded joiks with sounds of nature, such as reindeer bells, and encouraged other Sámi to joik. The entry also cites the "joik renaissance" of the 1970s, linked to the Sámi record company Jårgalæddji, which went bankrupt in the mid-1980s. Today joik appears in contemporary Sámi music, with names such as Mari Boine, who blends it with jazz and rock, and Ánde Somby, who joiks persons, animals and landscapes.

## In brief

Joik is a Sámi song that evokes a person, an animal or a place. It was condemned by the Church, almost disappeared and came back from 1968. Luohti is the style that stayed most alive.

## Terms

*Joik* is the traditional Sámi song. *Luohti*, *vuolle* and *vuelie* are regional names of joik. *Noaidi* is the Sámi shaman. *A cappella* is singing without instruments. *Assimilation* is the pressure on a people to give up their customs.

## What remains uncertain

The origin of joik is not documented: the entry says Sámi culture had no written language, and Nordics.info does not say when it arose. Oral tradition says that the fairies and elves of the Arctic gave joiks to the Sámi. The end of assimilation is given only as "relatively recently". The two texts differ a little, for example in saying which joik styles survive. And the entry marks some of its own sentences as lacking a source.
