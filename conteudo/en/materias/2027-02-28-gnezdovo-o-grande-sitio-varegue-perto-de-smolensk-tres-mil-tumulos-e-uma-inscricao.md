Gnezdovo is an archaeological site near the village of Gnezdovo, in Smolensk district, in Russia. This piece uses one source, an entry of an encyclopaedia in English, and notes where the entry itself warns that citations are missing or that there is debate among scholars.

## Where it is and what it was

According to the entry, the fortress stood at the confluence of the Dnieper and Svinets rivers, downstream from the portages between the Dvina and the Dnieper. It was a Slavic-Varangian settlement of the 10th century and a major trading post. The ring of rural settlements covers 17.5 hectares, and about 5,000 m² had been excavated by the end of the 20th century. The entry says it is one of the largest Viking Age sites surviving in Europe: only Hedeby was larger, with 24 hectares, and Birka had 13, Dublin 12, Ribe 10 and Gdańsk 1.

## The dates

The settlement flourished in the 10th century. It declined in the early 11th century, at the same time as other Varangian posts of eastern Europe. By the end of the 11th century, Smolensk had completely overtaken it as a trading centre.

## The mounds

There are about 3,000 burial mounds, in eight clusters. About 1,300 had been explored by Russian and Soviet archaeologists, since 1874. Of every 20 mounds, 19 hold ordinary burials of Krivichs and of Baltic men and women. The rite is mostly cremation, and the most numerous finds are household utensils and pottery. The metal finds include hauberks, helmets, battle-axes, Carolingian swords and arrows. The entry says the Varangian presence is pronounced, although scholars disagree about which ethnic group predominated.

## The Gnezdovo inscription

On a Kerch amphora there is the earliest inscription attested in Old East Slavic. The word inscribed in Cyrillic is *gorušna*. The excavator read it as mustard kept in the pot, but this reading is not accepted by everyone. The inscription is from the mid-10th century, and the latest coins in the same burial are from 906 or 907. Of the three runic inscriptions known from ancient Rus, only one, from Ladoga, predates it. The dating suggests that Cyrillic was more popular in pre-Christian Rus than was supposed.

## Trade and the Dnieper route

Seven hoards of Byzantine and Arabic coins have been found, including more than 1,100 Arabic coins. A Byzantine dish bears an image of Simargl. The site lay on the routes south to Constantinople and north, by portages, to the Dvina and Lovat rivers, with access to the Baltic. The fortress first served to defend the portages, where Norse traders were most vulnerable, and later was an exchange and refitting base on the route from the Baltic to the Black Sea.

## The research

The site drew the attention of archaeologists in 1867, when a hoard of 10th-century silver ornaments was found during the building of the Orel-Vitebsk railway. Proper investigation began in 1874, with M. F. Kustsinsky, and in the late 19th century V. I. Sizov excavated several hundred burial mounds. Research continues.

## In brief

Gnezdovo was a great trading post of the 10th century on the route between the Baltic and the Black Sea, with about 3,000 burial mounds and an inscription in Cyrillic from the middle of the century. It declined in the early 11th century, and Smolensk took its place.

## Terms

*Varangian* is the name given in Rus to the Scandinavians. *Kurgan* is a mound of earth over burials. *Cremation* is burning the body before burial. *Krivichs* are a Slavic people of the region. *Cyrillic* is the alphabet still used in Russian.

## What remains uncertain

The relationship with Smolensk is disputed: for some, Gnezdovo was the economic predecessor of Smolensk, and for others, Petrukhin and Pushkina, the two coexisted in the 10th century. Written sources on Gnezdovo are few and vague: two entries in the Primary Chronicle and one in *De Administrando Imperio*. The name Gnezdovo is late medieval, and the old name of the settlement is uncertain, with one proposal of Svinechsk. The "mustard" reading of the inscription is not universal, and the entry marks some comparisons as lacking citation.
