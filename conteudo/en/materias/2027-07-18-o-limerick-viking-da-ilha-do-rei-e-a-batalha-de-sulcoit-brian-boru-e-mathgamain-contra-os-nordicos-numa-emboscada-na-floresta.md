Limerick, today one of Ireland's main cities, began as a Viking settlement. The Norse settled on King's Island, an island in the River Shannon, probably in 922, first at Athlunkard. From there they controlled the estuary and threatened the interior. The Irish response came largely from one family: the Dál gCais.

## The Norse of the Shannon

The settlement lived from trade and piracy on the river. From the tenth century on, its best-known chief in the sources is Ímar of Limerick, who fought the local kings. For the Irish, the settlement was a point of attack; for the Norse, a power base on foreign soil.

## Sulcoit

The Battle of Sulcoit, today Soloheadbeg, in County Tipperary, is dated to 967 by the Annals of Inisfallen and of Ulster. Some accounts date it to 968. On one side were the Dál gCais, under Mathgamain and his brother Brian Boru. On the other, Ímar and his men. Tradition speaks of about a thousand Norse in mail, and says the Irish were fewer.

The most detailed account says the Norse were lured into woodland, where they could not form a shield wall. The Irish pursued them to Limerick and sacked it.

## A caution about the source

The liveliest account comes from the *Cogad Gáedel re Gallaib*, written more than a century later, and with a strong interest in exalting Brian Boru. Historians therefore use it with caution. The central fact, the victory of the Dál gCais, is accepted, but the numbers and details should be read as narrative.

## Why it matters

The battle gave Mathgamain and later Brian a power base. Brian would become High King of Ireland and die at Clontarf, in 1014. Limerick continued as a city.

Between the probable founding of the settlement, in 922, and the Battle of Sulcoit, in 967, about forty-five years passed. That is enough time for the settlement to become a power on the river, and for a neighbouring Irish family, the Dál gCais, to see in it an obstacle and a prize.

The episode shows, in small, how the history of the Vikings in Ireland was written. On one side, the annals, which record dates and results in short sentences. On the other, later chronicles, which add colour, numbers and speeches. Anyone who wants to know what happened has to separate what is in the annals from what came afterwards, and accept that the rest remains open.
