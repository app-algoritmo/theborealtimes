The *draugr* is, according to the source consulted, a dead person of the Icelandic sagas who does not stay in the grave. What the text brings are cases from different sagas, and each shows one facet.

## The sources

In *Grettis saga*, Kárr the Old is explicitly called a draugr and is entombed in a mound that reeks horribly. Glámr, in the same saga, is a ghost the text never calls a draugr, although modern scholars often do. In *Laxdæla saga*, Víga-Hrappr roams his farmstead instead of staying in the grave. He can shapeshift into a seal and sink into the ground to escape. In *Eyrbyggja saga*, Þórólfr bægifótr, "Lame-foot", spreads a deadly haunting, and the "door-doom" is used to expel draugar from a house. There is also Þráinn, in *Hrómundar saga Gripssonar*, and, in *Harðar saga*, a barrow with a deadly stench.

## The look

The article gives the draugr superhuman strength, a death-blue or corpse-pale appearance and a strong stench of decay. Glámr's body was found swollen and dark.

## Glámr

Glámr died on Christmas Eve and afterwards caused havoc, driving some people to faint or lose their minds. Grettir defeated him in a wrestling struggle, and Glámr cursed him before dying.

## How to deal with a draugr

Weapons often fail, and heroes tend to wrestle the draugr back into its grave. Iron can wound it, but usually does not stop it. The usual remedy is to behead the body, burn it and scatter the ashes at sea, so that it does not return. Breaking the posture of a seated or upright corpse is sometimes necessary, but risks the evil eye.

## The mounds

Draugar usually dwell in burial mounds, often guarding treasure. A related figure, the *haugbúi*, "mound-dweller", cannot leave the grave and attacks only those who trespass. A draugr's presence may be marked by a glow coming from the mound, like foxfire.

## Terms

*Draugr* is the dead who walks; *haugbúi* is the mound-dweller, who attacks only those who trespass; the "door-doom" is the expulsion procedure of *Eyrbyggja saga*, which the article only mentions, without describing.

## What remains uncertain

The article warns that the text of *Grettis saga* does not call Glámr a draugr, and that modern scholars do so on their own. This shows that the category "draugr" is, in part, a modern convention applied to different accounts. The sagas have different authors and periods, and do not form a single body of belief.
