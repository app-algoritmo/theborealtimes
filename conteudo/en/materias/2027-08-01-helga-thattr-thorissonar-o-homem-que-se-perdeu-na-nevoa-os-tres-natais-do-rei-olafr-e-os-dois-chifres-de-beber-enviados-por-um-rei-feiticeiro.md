The *Helga þáttr Þórissonar* is one of the strangest tales in the *Flateyjarbók*, in the section about King Óláfr Tryggvason. It mixes a trip to Finnmark, a princess from a realm of another world, a poisoned gift and a bishop's blessing.

## The foggy forest

Helgi Þórisson and his brother make a trading voyage to Finnmark. On the way back, Helgi gets lost in a forest wrapped in fog and meets Ingibjörg, daughter of King Guðmundr of Glæsisvellir, with eleven attendants dressed in red and riding red horses. Helgi spends three nights with her. On parting, she gives him two chests, one of gold and one of silver, and warns him not to tell anyone where they came from.

## The first Christmas

Back in Norway, the brothers live with their father, Þórir, near the Oslofjord. Helgi spends part of the gold on decorating the ship and hides the rest in the dragon-shaped prow. On a stormy night, as he goes to check the ship, two men suddenly appear with a great crash and carry him off. Þórir reports what happened to King Óláfr.

## The second Christmas

A year later, on the eighth day of Christmas, Óláfr's court is at Alreksstaðir. Three men enter: Helgi and two who call themselves Grímr. They say that King Guðmundr has sent, as a sign of respect and friendship, two magnificent drinking horns, also called Grímr. Óláfr finds them finer than his own, the Hyrningar. He has them filled with ale and a bishop blesses them. On seeing the blessing, the visitors spill the ale, put out the lights and vanish with a crash. Three of the king's men are found dead beside the horns. Óláfr concludes that Guðmundr is a dangerous sorcerer and has the horns kept.

## The third Christmas

During mass on the eighth day of Christmas, two men leave a blind, thin Helgi at the church door. He explains that the Grímr were sent to harm the king, but the blessing prevented them, and that Guðmundr released him because of Óláfr's prayers. Ingibjörg had blinded him when he left. Helgi stays with the king for the rest of his life, which lasts a year. Óláfr has the horns with him when he dies in the battle of Svöldr.

## What remains uncertain

The tale links the realm of Glæsisvellir, which appears in other legendary sagas as a land beyond the known world, to the cycle of Óláfr. Scholars think it was already in the immediate source of the compiler of the *Flateyjarbók*, Jón Þórðarson. The date of composition is not secure.
