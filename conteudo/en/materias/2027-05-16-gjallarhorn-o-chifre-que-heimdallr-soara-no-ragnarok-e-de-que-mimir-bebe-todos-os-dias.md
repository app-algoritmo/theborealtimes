The name Gjallarhorn is usually glossed as "the resounding horn" or "the loud-sounding horn". Lindow defines the term as a long brass instrument, something like an unvalved trumpet. In Norse mythology it belongs to Heimdallr, and its sound will be heard in all worlds.

## Three mentions in the Prose Edda

Snorri Sturluson names the horn three times, always in the Gylfaginning. In chapter 15, describing the roots of the ash Yggdrasil, he says one of them reaches Mímir's well, and that Mímir "is full of learning because he drinks of the well from the horn Gjallarhorn". In chapter 27, Heimdallr appears as the owner of the horn, whose sound is heard in all worlds.

In chapter 51, the one on Ragnarök, the enemies of the gods gather on the plain of Vígríðr. Heimdallr stands and blows the horn mightily, and the gods awake and assemble at the thing.

## What the poems say

The Poetic Edda is more economical. The Völuspá says that the horn, or, in another reading, Heimdallr's "hearing" (the word hljóð can mean both), is hidden under the holy tree. The translation of the line is disputed for that reason.

## A horn to drink from, a horn to blow

Rudolf Simek notes that a horn that serves as instrument and cup is not odd: Roland's Olifant is a parallel. He adds that the horn is one of the oldest Germanic instruments, along with the lurs, and that finds like the 5th-century Golden Horns of Gallehus suggest sacral horns existed, perhaps the earthly versions of Gjallarhorn.

Some scholars read a figure on a stone from the Isle of Man and another on the Gosforth Cross, of the 9th and 10th centuries, as Heimdallr with the horn, but these identifications are hypotheses.

## In short

In the sources Gjallarhorn does two things: it gives wisdom to whoever drinks from it, in this case Mímir, and it announces the end of the world. To play in the right rhythm, play [Heimdallr's blast](/game-heimdallr-s-blast/).
