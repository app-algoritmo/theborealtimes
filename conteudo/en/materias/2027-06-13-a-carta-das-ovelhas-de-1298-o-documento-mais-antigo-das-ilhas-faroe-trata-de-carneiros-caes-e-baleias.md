The Seyðabrævið ("Sheep Letter", in Old Norse *sauðabréfit*) is the oldest surviving document of the Faroe Islands. According to the sources, it is a royal decree of 28 June 1298. It deals mainly with sheep farming, but has other subjects and functioned as a kind of constitution.

## Who issued it

The decree is by Duke Haakon, who later became King Haakon V of Norway. It was drafted on the advice of Erlend, bishop of the diocese of the Faroe Islands, at Kirkjubøur, and of Sjúrður, the lawspeaker of Shetland. Haakon had sent Sjúrður to the Faroes to examine deficiencies in the agricultural law.

## What it regulates

According to the article's list, the letter deals with: proof of ownership before sheep are slaughtered; trespass and grazing on others' land; taming wild sheep; ear-marking, where altering the first mark counted as theft; dogs that bite sheep, with compensation and pasture stocking limits; deadlines when authorities issue warnings; renting land; unwanted visitors and the rights of the poor; testimony; payment for visitors who stay; households and tenants; whale finds and the division of meat and blubber; and flotsam.

## Why it matters

Politically, it moved most administrative power from the local Thing to the king and his representatives. Because it was written in the Faroes, unlike the *Færeyinga saga*, which is Icelandic, it gives a better picture of Faroese society at the time. It also shows early changes in the Faroese language from Old Norse.

## The manuscript

Two copies survive: one in the National Archives of the Faroe Islands, in Tórshavn, and the other in the library of Lund University, in Sweden. The source consulted gives no further details of the document's later history.
