The *Karlamagnús saga* is one of the most extensive works of medieval Norse literature. It was compiled in the late thirteenth century in Norway and gathers, in ten branches, stories about Charlemagne and his twelve paladins. The material comes from varied origins: Latin historiography and French chansons de geste.

## The ten branches

The branches deal, in turn, with:

1. The beginning of the story of Charlemagne (*Upphaf Karlamagnúss*).
2. Olif and her son Landres.
3. Oddgeir the Dane, from the chanson de geste *La Chevalerie Ogier de Danemarche*.
4. King Agulandus, the longest branch, which joins the *Historia Caroli Magni* and a version of the *Chanson d'Aspremont*.
5. Gvitalin the Saxon, a campaign against the Saxons recalling Jean Bodel's *Chanson de Saisnes*.
6. Otuel, a version of the *Chanson d'Otinel*.
7. The journey to Jerusalem, a very close translation of an Anglo-Norman manuscript of the *Pèlerinage de Charlemagne*.
8. The battle of Runzival, a version of the *Song of Roland*.
9. William Short-Nose, from the *Moniage Guillaume*, of the Guillaume d'Orange cycle.
10. Miracles and signs, or the death of Charlemagne, based on Vincent of Beauvais's *Speculum historiale*.

## The Agulandus branch

Branch IV is by far the largest. Charlemagne and Roland fight Agulandus, his son Jamund and the giant Ferragut. The *Agulandus þáttr* shows class tensions breaking down during the war against the "infidels," and Saracen warriors panic the Christian cavalry.

## Roland in Norse

Branch VIII is textually close to the Oxford manuscript of the *Song of Roland*, with a few notable differences, and served as the basis of the Norwegian ballad *Roland og Magnus kongen*. The name *Karlamagnús* comes from Latin *Carolus Magnus*, and the ballad uses a similar form, Magnus, for the king.

## The work's fortunes

A Danish abridgment, whose earliest manuscript dates from 1480, and a fourteenth-century Swedish verse translation show that the saga was read outside Norway.
