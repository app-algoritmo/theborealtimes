Olaf III Haraldsson, the youngest son of Harald Hardrada, is remembered by a nickname that sounds unheroic: *Kyrre*, in Old Norse *kyrri*, "the Peaceful". He reigned from 1067 until his death on 22 September 1093, and the sources draw attention precisely to what he did not do. He organised no great expeditions of conquest. He worked to protect Norway's sovereignty through agreements and marriages.

## The young man who guarded the ships

In 1066, at sixteen, Olaf accompanied his father in the invasion of England. At the battle of Stamford Bridge, on 25 September, he stayed at Riccall guarding the ships and did not fight. After the defeat he wintered in Orkney and returned to Norway in the summer of 1067. He reigned together with his brother Magnus II until the latter's death in 1069, and alone from then on. He made peace with William the Conqueror and married Ingerid, daughter of the Danish king Svend Estridsen. The marriage produced no children, but the king had an illegitimate son, who would become Magnus III, Barefoot.

## Bergen and the churches

Tradition, going back to Snorri Sturluson, credits Olaf with founding Bergen, then Bjørgvin, around 1070. The king's measures made the towns, above all Bergen, better royal residences. Bishops came to have fixed seats in Oslo, Nidaros and Bergen. In Bergen he began Christ Church, and in Nidaros, today's Trondheim, around 1070 he began a large stone church dedicated to the Holy Trinity, on the site of an older wooden church. That building was about fifty metres long, the largest in Norway at the time. The altar stood over the grave of Saint Olaf, the king's half-uncle, half-brother of Harald, and when the work was finished, around 1090, the saint's shrine was moved there. Olaf, unlike his father, fully recognised the authority of the Archbishopric of Bremen over the Norwegian Church.

## Court, guilds and law

The king strengthened royal power and reorganised the personal guard, the *húskarlar*. He instituted the guild system in Norway. It was also during his reign that the Gulating laws were probably first written down. A Norwegian silver coin, the *Maine penny*, found in the United States in 1957, has been dated to his time, although no one knows how it got there.

## A silent king, but merry at the table

*Heimskringla* describes him as silent in general, but "merry in drinking parties", and says he was peaceful throughout his reign. *Morkinskinna*, from about 1220, says something similar: a taciturn man, good company after a few drinks. Olaf died of illness at Haukbø, in Rånrike, today's Håkeby in Sweden, and was buried in Nidaros Cathedral.

## What remains

The saga of Olaf Kyrre was written around 1225, more than a century later, and gives a favourable picture of the king. But a Norway without foreign wars, with bishops in fixed seats and towns growing, helps explain why, after him, the quarrels over the crown were internal.
