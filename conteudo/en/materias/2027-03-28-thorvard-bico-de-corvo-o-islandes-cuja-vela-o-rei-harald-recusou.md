Many *þættir*, the short Icelandic tales, turn around a gift. In this one, the object is a ship's sail. The Icelander Thorvard offers it to King Harald, who refuses it out of superstition, and whoever accepts it ends up with the best of sails. I read the text in Eiður Eyþórsson's translation.

## The Icelander and the sail

Thorvard, called "Crow-Nose" (*krákunefr*), is a wealthy Icelander of good character, from a family of the Westfjords, who travels widely. He sails to Nidaros and offers King Harald a sail from his ship. The king refuses. He recalls that he had once accepted a sail from Icelanders, which split and brought him trouble. His words in the tale are: "Once I accepted a sail from you Icelanders, and suspected it would bring me trouble."

## The king's son-in-law

The one who accepts the sail is Eystein Orri, the king's son-in-law and the son of Thorberg Arnason, described as the bravest of men and highly honoured by Harald. Eystein admires the sail and invites Thorvard to spend the winter on his estate, at Gizka. In spring, Thorvard goes there, receives generous hospitality and is delayed by the feasts and by the favourable winds Eystein makes wait.

## The gifts

In return, Eystein gives Thorvard three gifts: a richly embroidered scarlet tunic, a grey fur cloak with a scarlet mantle and a gold ring. He says that the cloak is the true payment for the sail, and that "this will not be the payment for the sail". Thorvard returns to Iceland and gains great standing.

## The sail returns to the king

Later, the king sees Eystein's sail racing ahead and realizes it is the one he refused. Eystein offers him the choice of sails, and the king keeps it. Even on a larger ship, the sail keeps pace with the royal ship, and the king says he has never seen a better one. He regrets having declined "something fine".

## In short

The tale shows that refusing a gift can be an expensive mistake and that whoever knows how to receive is rewarded. Thorvard's sail passes from the king to the son-in-law and back to the king, with a ring, a tunic and a cloak along the way.

## Terms

A *þáttr* (plural *þættir*) is a short Icelandic tale. *Nidaros* is medieval Trondheim. *Flateyjarbók* is a large Icelandic manuscript of the late fourteenth century.

## What remains uncertain

The tale is a literary narrative, and the sources I read do not say whether Thorvard, the sail or the race existed. The translation says the king is Harald and gives no more detail. They also do not explain the nickname "Crow-Nose".
