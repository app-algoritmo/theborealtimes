*Fagrskinna* is a history of the kings of Norway, written in Old Norse. The source consulted dates it to about 1220 and says the author is unknown: it is often thought to have been written in Norway, by an Icelander or by a Norwegian.

## What it covers

The text narrates the history of Norway from the 9th to the 12th century, from the career of Halfdan the Black to the battle of Re in 1177. The article says it emphasizes battles and cites many skaldic verses, some of which are preserved nowhere else. That preservation is part of the work's value: the verses are a form of record from the period that the surrounding prose only arranges.

## The name

*Fagrskinna* means "Fair Leather", or "Fair Parchment". The article explains that the name comes from one of the manuscripts that preserved it, and refers to the type of parchment.

## The sources and the relatives

*Morkinskinna*, from about 1220, is one of the written sources proposed as most decisive for *Fagrskinna*. The article also places it as an immediate source of Snorri Sturluson's *Heimskringla*. In addition, *Fagrskinna* probably drew on a lost version of the *Ágrip af Nóregs konunga sögum*, from about 1190, one among several proposed sources.

## The manuscripts

The original manuscript was destroyed by fire. Copies of it and of another parchment survived, and the text reaches us by that route.

## Terms

*Skald* is the Norse court poet; *kings' saga* is a prose narrative about kings; *Ágrip* is an earlier history, from about 1190, which the article cites as a possible source; the *battle of Re* is the point at which the account ends.

## Four texts in relation

The article places *Fagrskinna* in the middle of a small family of texts. The *Ágrip*, from about 1190, appears as a possible source. *Morkinskinna*, from about 1220, is named as a decisive written source. Snorri Sturluson's *Heimskringla* uses *Fagrskinna* as an immediate source. Anyone reading one of these texts is therefore also reading echoes of the others, and the similarities between them need to be read with that in mind.

## Why the verses matter

Since the work cites many skaldic verses, and some exist only in it, losing *Fagrskinna* would mean losing those verses. The article does not detail how many there are, or which, and this piece does not invent numbers.

## What remains uncertain

The author has not been identified, and the origin in Norway or Iceland is not settled. The main sources are "proposed", not confirmed. And since the original is lost, the text exists only in copies, which means reading the work with the caution one has before any text that arrived at second hand.
