The word *elf* today suggests fairies and children's tales, but in the Norse sources the *álfar* are beings of another kind, and their land has a name: Álfheimr. Little was written about it, and that is exactly why the two passages where it appears deserve a close reading.

## Only two mentions

Old Norse texts speak of Álfheimr in just two places. The first is the poem Grímnismál, in the Poetic Edda, which describes twelve divine dwellings. Stanza 5 says that the gods gave Álfheimr to the god Freyr "in days of old", as a *tannfé*, a "tooth-gift". This tooth-gift was given to a child when the first tooth came, and the study of the poem links it to the honouring of a first sign of growth. In Larrington's translation, the stanza says the gods gave Álfheimr to Freyr "in bygone days as tooth-payment". The same stretch mentions Ullr's dwelling in Ýdalir just before.

## Snorri's elves

The second mention is by Snorri Sturluson, in the Prose Edda, written around 1220. In Gylfaginning he lists Álfheimr first among the dwellings of heaven. The light elves live there. Snorri says they are "fairer to look upon than the sun". The dark elves, he says, live under the earth, and are "blacker than pitch". Snorri then speaks of a hall called Gimlé, at the highest point of heaven, which is meant to survive the end of heaven and earth. That suggests that only the light elves inhabit these high places.

## Light and shadow

According to some scholars, the division is too tidy to be old, and they suspect that Snorri arranged into two groups what in popular belief was looser. Others note that Snorri's dark elves look very much like the dwarfs, who also live under the earth. Either way, it is Snorri's picture, those above and those below, that became the best known.

## In short

Álfheimr is the home of the light elves, given to Freyr as a tooth-gift, according to Grímnismál. Snorri places it in heaven and sets it against the underground world of the dark elves.

## Terms

*Álfar* is the plural of *álfr*, "elf". *Tannfé* is the gift made for a child's first tooth. *Gylfaginning* is the part of the Prose Edda that tells how King Gylfi was deceived.

## What remains uncertain

With only two mentions, context is missing. We do not know how people in the 9th and 10th centuries pictured elves, or whether the split into light and dark was old or Snorri's own creation. The relation between the dark elves and the dwarfs is also unresolved.
