In Norse mythology, the wind has an owner. Two sources say that it comes from the wings of a giant in the shape of an eagle, named Hræsvelgr, who sits at the end of the sky. It is a short image, but it shows how the Norse imagined the sky: as a place with edges, where beings from outside the world of the gods live.

## Odin's question

In the *Vafþrúðnismál*, a poem of the Poetic Edda, Odin disguises himself as a traveller called Gagnráðr and visits the giant Vafþrúðnir for a contest of knowledge. Whoever gets an answer wrong loses his head. Among the questions, Odin wants to know where the wind comes from. The giant answers that it is Hræsvelgr, a giant who sits at the end of the sky in the shape of an eagle, and that from his wings, they say, the wind passes over all peoples. This is stanza 37.

## What Snorri adds

In the *Gylfaginning*, Gangleri, the disguised King Gylfi, asks Hár where the wind comes from. The answer repeats the idea of the poem and completes it: Hræsvelgr sits at the northern end of the sky, in the shape of an eagle, and when he spreads his wings to fly, the wind is born beneath them. ## The name

The name Hræsvelgr is usually translated as "corpse-swallower", according to Orchard (1997) and Lindow (2002). Lindow also records another reading, something like "shipwreck-current". The name speaks of the dead, not of wind, and the page I consulted does not explain that difference. It only cites a 1998 article by Jón Hnefill Aðalsteinsson, "Hræsvelgr, the Wind-Giant, Reinterpreted", without summarising what he argues.

## A giant, not a god

The poem calls Hræsvelgr a giant, a *jötunn*. It does not say that anyone honours him or that he is a god. The wind comes, then, from a being that tradition places at the edges of the world, not from one of the Æsir. The source also does not say why he sits at the far end of the sky, or where he blows.

## The contest in which he appears

The passage is part of a contest of questions and answers about the world. The other questions in the poem deal, for example, with the horses that draw the day and the night, with the rivers and with the end of the world. At the end, Odin asks a question that only he can answer, about what he whispered to his son Baldr on the pyre, and that is how he wins.

## In short

Hræsvelgr is a giant in the shape of an eagle who, at the end of the sky, makes the wind with his wings. He appears in two sources, the *Vafþrúðnismál* and the *Gylfaginning*, and the second follows the poem closely.

## Terms

*Jötunn* is the Norse word for giant. *Poetic Edda* is the collection of poems about gods and heroes preserved in a thirteenth-century manuscript. *Prose Edda* is Snorri Sturluson's work on the myths and on poetry.

## What remains uncertain

No source says why the name speaks of corpses. The page consulted does not give the chapter of the *Gylfaginning* and does not summarise the 1998 article. It is probable, but not proved, that Snorri took the image from the poem itself.
