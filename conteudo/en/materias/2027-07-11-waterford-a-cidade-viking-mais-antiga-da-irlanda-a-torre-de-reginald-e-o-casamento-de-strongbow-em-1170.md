Waterford lies in south-eastern Ireland, on the south bank of the river Suir, near where it reaches the sea. The city is proud to be the oldest in the country, and the reason is direct: it began as a Viking port. The Irish annals point to around 914 for the arrival of a Norse fleet on the river, and that date is the one most used for the founding. The name comes from Old Norse and means something like "ram fjord" (Veðrafjörðr).

## The port on the river

Like other Viking towns in Ireland, Dublin and Limerick among them, Waterford stood where a navigable river met the sea. It was a base for trade and raiding, and later a town of merchants who had both agreements and quarrels with the Irish kings of the region. The old part of the city is still called the Viking Triangle, because of the shape drawn by its streets and quays.

## Reginald's Tower

Reginald's Tower is a round stone tower at the corner of the quay. According to tradition, the name comes from Ragnall, one of the Norse leaders from the city's early days. The present stone building is generally dated to the 13th century, not to the Viking age, and it now houses a museum. In its long life it served as fortress, prison, storehouse and mint.

## 1170: Strongbow and Aoife

In 1170 the Normans came to Ireland at the request of Diarmait Mac Murchada, king of Leinster. One of them, Richard de Clare, known as Strongbow, took Waterford after a short fight. In the town itself, according to the sources, he married Aoife, Diarmait's daughter, in an agreement that would give him rights in Leinster. The city came to be ruled by Norman lords, and later by the kings of England.
