Herding hares sounds like an impossible task, which is why the king offers it as a test. In the tale "Osborn's Pipe", from Peter Christen Asbjørnsen's collection in George Webbe Dasent's translation, whoever passes the test gets the reward of the kingdom, and whoever fails pays dearly. Only one brother reaches the end, and he does so with the help of a pipe.

## The three brothers

A poor farmer loses his farm, and his three idle sons, Peter, Paul and Osborn Boots, go to seek work as keepers of the king's hares. Peter and Paul mock an old woman whose nose is stuck in a log. Both lose the hares, and the king punishes them with red stripes and a pit of snakes.

## The pipe

Osborn frees the old woman, shares his food with her and receives a magic pipe. One end scatters whatever he wishes, and the other gathers everything back. With it he keeps the hares easily. The maid, the princess and the queen each try to buy the pipe, and each pays with kisses. Osborn wishes the pipe back into his hands every time, and the buyers are left without it.

## The king and the vat

The king himself goes after Osborn to buy the pipe. Osborn demands that the king kiss his white horse, with a handkerchief between them, and the king gets the pipe, only to lose it again. Furious, the king condemns Osborn to death unless he can fill a brewing vat with lies. Osborn tells of all the trickery he has lived through, and the vat overflows. The king declares the vat full, Osborn marries the princess and gets half the kingdom. As he himself says at the end: "That was something like a pipe."

## What the tale shows

The plot is typical of the Norwegian collected tale: three brothers, two who fail through scorn and one who wins through kindness. The pipe works as a two-ended object, one end that scatters and one that gathers, and the hero uses it to beat each buyer without violence. The final challenge of the vat of lies turns the hero's cleverness into a proof of worth.

## In short

Osborn, the youngest of three brothers, helps an old woman, gets a pipe that scatters and gathers, keeps the king's hares, beats the king and queen in the trade of kisses and marries the princess after filling a vat with lies.

## Terms

A *pipe* is the instrument of the tale, which here is also a magic object. A *vat* is a large wooden container. *Dasent* is the English translator of the collections of Asbjørnsen and Moe.

## What remains uncertain

We do not know how this tale circulated before it was collected, nor how it was told by word of mouth. Dasent's translation, published in English, may have changed details and names of the Norwegian version.
