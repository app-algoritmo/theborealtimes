In southern Jutland, in the region that today lies between Denmark and Germany, there is a line of earth-and-timber ramparts that the Danes called Danevirke, "work of the Danes". It was not built all at once, but extended and rebuilt over centuries. Today, stretches of it can be seen as long grass-covered rises.

## What the chronicles say

The first written mention appears in the *Royal Frankish Annals*, in the entry for the year 808. The text says that the Danish king Godfred gathered his forces and had a rampart built on the border with the Saxons, stretching from a bay of the Baltic to the North Sea. According to the account, there was a single gate for carts and horsemen, and the aim was to protect the territory against Frankish attacks.

The context is the expansion of Charlemagne, who had conquered Saxony after long wars. With this, the Frankish Empire came to share a direct border with the Danes, and King Godfred answered with a military work.

## What the trees say

The timber used in the ramparts allows the phases of the work to be dated by dendrochronology, which compares the growth rings of trees. The analyses indicate that the first structures of the complex are older than the Frankish account, with trunks felled around 737. A major enlargement, which included new stretches and reinforcements, has been dated to about 968, and many researchers link it to King Harald Bluetooth, who also had circular fortresses built. Later, in the 12th century, King Valdemar I had a brick wall added, one of the first constructions in that material in Denmark.

The whole complex is about thirty kilometres long, and was used and rebuilt for more than a thousand years. In the war of 1864, the Danevirke still served as a defensive position for the Danish army, which withdrew from it under Prussian pressure.

## A complex tied to Hedeby

The Danevirke is linked to the port of Hedeby, a trading town founded around the 8th century. In 2018, UNESCO recognised the two sites as one ensemble, called the Archaeological Border Complex of Hedeby and the Danevirke. Specialists point out that the work had more than one function: it served defence, controlled traffic between north and south and showed the power of whoever had it raised.

It is wise to be careful with the idea of a "great Viking wall". The dates, the phases and the builders of each stretch are still debated, and many attributions to specific kings are hypotheses.

The Danevirke is also a good example of how archaeology and writing complete each other. The chronicles give a year and a name, the timber gives other dates, and the earth shows how the palisades were made. When the three sources coincide, the result is firmer. When they diverge, researchers must explain why such a large work may have been rebuilt so many times.
