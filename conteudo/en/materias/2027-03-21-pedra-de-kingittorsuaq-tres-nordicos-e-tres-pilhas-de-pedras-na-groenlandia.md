Almost all Norse runestones are in Scandinavia, but one of them lies much farther away: on top of an island in northern Greenland, among piles of stones. The Kingittorsuaq stone is one of the few written signs the Norse left so far from home, and it still holds a passage that nobody can read.

## Where and when it was found

The stone was found in 1824 on the island of Kingittorsuaq, in the Upernavik archipelago, in northwest Greenland. It lay in a group of three piles of stones, or *varðar*, which formed an equilateral triangle on the top of the island. Today it is kept in the National Museum of Denmark, in Copenhagen.

## What the inscription says

The text names three men: Erlingr Sighvatsson, Bjarni Þórðarson and Eindriði Oddsson. It says they built these cairns on the Saturday before Gagndagr, the "procession day", or Rogation Day, and then comes a passage that is partly undeciphered. The writing is in medieval runes, with bind runes, in which a single sign combines two letters, as in the sequence *ar*.

## The date

The stone is dated to the Middle Ages, generally around 1300. Other proposed dates range from 1250 to 1333. The historian Finn Gad observed that the date can be read in various ways and that, for this reason, it does not prove that the three hunters spent the winter there.

## The six runes nobody reads

The last six runes remain undeciphered, and every scholar has proposed something. Rasmus Rask, in 1824, thought they encoded the year. Finnur Jónsson, in 1914, took them for ornament or magical signs. Magnus Olsen, in 1932, suggested the last word was *rýndu*, "wrote runes". Fritz Läffler, in 1906, read *ruddu* and the magic word *ís*, "ice", in the sense of "cleared the ice". William Thalbitzer, in 1951, joined the two ideas in *ok rýndu ís*. In 1967, Alf Mongé proposed that the runes held two sets of ciphers, one for a date and one for the carver's name, but Mindy MacLeod, in 2002, found little or no evidence that bind runes were used for numerical magic.

## In short

The Kingittorsuaq stone, found in 1824, records that three Norsemen made three piles of stones on an island in northwest Greenland, around 1300. The final six runes remain unread.

## Terms

A *varða* (plural *varðar*) is a pile of stones used as a marker. A *bind rune* is a sign that joins two letters. *Gagndagr* is procession day, tied to the Christian Rogation days.

## What remains uncertain

We do not know what the last runes say, or whether the date was written in cipher. The source consulted also does not say who found the stone, and the context of what the three men were doing so far north is, in good part, deduction.
