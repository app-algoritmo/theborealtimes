The Ravning Enge bridge, in Danish *Ravningbroen*, was a timber bridge in the Vejle river valley in Jutland, Denmark. It lay about ten kilometres south of Jelling, near the village of Ravning, and crossed the meadows of Ravning Enge. According to the first source, it was the longest bridge in Denmark until the Little Belt Bridge was built in 1935. Today a reconstructed span exists. This piece uses two sources, an encyclopaedia in English and the text of the Viking Ship Museum, and separates what they state from what they leave open.

## The measurements

The encyclopaedia gives about 760 metres in length, a width of 5 to 5.5 metres and at least 1.5 metres in height. The structure was of oak, with 280 spans of 2.4 metres. Each pier had four vertical posts, about 30 by 30 centimetres and up to 6 metres long, and the deck covered about 3,800 square metres. Building it is estimated to have required felling more than 300 hectares of oak forest. The museum speaks of "at least" 760 metres, a width of 5 metres and almost 1,800 oak posts "felled with axes", and says that most remain in place, protected under a low embankment.

## The dating

A piece of oak dated by radiocarbon gave the year 980, with an uncertainty of 100 years. The dendrochronological analysis by the National Museum of Denmark concluded that the timber was felled in the 980s, probably in the first half of the decade. Another dendrochronologist questioned the end of that interval and concluded that one can only say "after c. 980 and before c. 1010".

## Who had it built

Both sources date the work to around 980 and attribute it to King Harald Bluetooth, who also had the ring fortresses built. The encyclopaedia says the bridge, like those fortresses, was in use only briefly, but marks that claim as lacking a citation. The museum says it was in use for about 50 years.

## What it was for

The purpose is much debated. Excavations in 1993 and 1996 indicated that it served as a trading bridge and as a jetty for ships. In the Viking Age, water levels in the valley were higher, and ships could sail further inland and dock closer to the royal seat at Jelling. The width allowed two trading wagons to pass each other. The museum adds that the king may have collected tolls, which it presents as possible, and that the bridge let the king's guards respond quickly to threats from the south.

## Terms

*Dendrochronology* is dating by counting the growth rings of wood. *Radiocarbon* is a dating technique based on the decay of carbon. A *pier* is each vertical support of the bridge. A *meadow* here means low, damp ground.

## What remains uncertain

The sources differ on the exact length, the width, the distance to Jelling (about 10 kilometres in one, 14 in the other), the year of discovery (1953 in one, 1934 in the other), the excavation campaigns (1972 plus 1993 and 1996, against the 1970s and 1990s) and the length of use. The date of the timber retains a margin, and the toll function is only a possibility.
