In Norse mythology the sun and the moon are not just heavenly bodies: they are two figures running across the sky, and each has a wolf behind it. The story appears in two main sources, the *Prose Edda* of Snorri Sturluson and the poems of the *Poetic Edda*, and the two do not always say the same thing.

## Two siblings in the sky

According to the *Gylfaginning*, there was a man called Mundilfari who had two children so beautiful that he named them Sól, "sun", and Máni, "moon". The gods were angered by the father's pride and took the two into the sky. Sól came to drive the horses that pull the chariot of the sun, and Máni came to steer the moon and govern its phases. The father's name also appears in the poem *Vafþrúðnismál*, which suggests the idea was old.

The *Grímnismál* adds a curious detail. The horses of the sun are called Árvakr and Alsviðr, and in front of them the gods placed a shield called Svalinn, "the cool one". According to the poem, without the shield the heat of the sun would burn up the sea and the mountains. It is a poetic explanation for why the sun warms the world without destroying it.

## The wolves that give chase

Snorri says that Sól runs fast because she is being pursued, and that the wolf Sköll follows her. Máni, in turn, is followed by Hati, son of Hróðvitnir. The sources link the wolves to a giantess who lived in the forest of Járnviðr, "iron wood". When the end of the world comes, at Ragnarök, the wolves will finally catch the two bodies, and the sky will go dark. Snorri says that the wolf called Mánagarmr will gorge on the flesh of the dead and stain the sky with blood.

The same text mentions two children, Bil and Hjúki, whom Máni is said to have taken from the earth as they carried a bucket on a pole. Many scholars think this image may be tied to the markings on the moon, but the link is a hypothesis, and Snorri does not state it.

## What is known and what is not

The *Vafþrúðnismál* says that, before she is swallowed, Sól will have a daughter, and that the daughter will follow the same road as her mother when the gods die. The passage is one of the few that speak of a world continuing after Ragnarök, and it is often cited by those who study the Norse view of time.

It is worth remembering that in Old Norse *sól* is feminine and *máni* is masculine. This is the reverse of what happens in many languages of southern Europe, and some researchers see in it a trait of the Germanic tradition. It is also worth remembering that Snorri was a 13th-century Christian writing to preserve old poetry. His explanations may have been arranged by him, and may not come from a belief everyone shared.
