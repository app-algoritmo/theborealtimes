In Denmark, the Mosekonen, "the bog woman", is the one who makes the mist that rises from the marshes and meadows after warm days. She was said to be brewing, and the mist was the "Mosekonen's brew". When the mist settled, she stopped fermenting. The best-known source for this figure is Hans Christian Andersen, who put her in a tale published on 17 November 1865.

## The tale

"The will-o'-the-wisps are in town, says the Bog Woman" appears in the collection Nye Eventyr og Historier. A man who once knew many tales finds that they no longer visit him, after a war. He looks for them in the city, in books, in flowers and in the countryside, without finding them. At night, the Mosekonen taps at his window and warns that the lygtemænd, the bog lights, are loose in town. The man half believes her, but fears that nobody will take the warning seriously.

## A troll who was an elf

In the story, the Mosekonen describes herself as a troll-being who was once an elf girl. She keeps a cabinet of "poetry in bottles", an heirloom from the Devil's great-grandmother, with the essence of various poets and genres. She tends a barrel, and when a tap falls out, the beer runs off.

## The twelve lygtemænd

In the tale, twelve lygtemænd are born in the bog in a single night. For one year, each can take human form and lead 365 people astray. The one who succeeds earns a fiery place as the Devil's runner. The one who fails is stuck, shining, in rotten wood. In popular belief, the lygtemænd are lights over the bogs that draw people off their path.

## The fire that marks a treasure

Danes, Finns, Swedes and other peoples believed that the bog light marked a buried treasure, which could be taken only while the fire was burning. Science attributes the light to the oxidation of phosphine, diphosphane and methane, gases that come from the decay of organic matter.
