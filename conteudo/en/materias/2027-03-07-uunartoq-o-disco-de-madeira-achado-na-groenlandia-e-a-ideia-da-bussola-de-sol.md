The Uunartoq disc is a fragment of a wooden disc, with triangular notches on the rim and a hole in the centre. This piece follows the article "Uunartoq Disc", which uses careful language, with phrases such as "generally accepted" and "appears to", and that shows here too.

## The find

In 1948, the Danish archaeologist Christen Vebæk found the disc while excavating site Ø149, on the western shore of a fjord that the Norse called Siglufjord and that is now called Uunartoq, in Norse Greenland. The site has a church that is generally accepted to be the Benedictine convent mentioned by Ivar Bardarson in the mid-14th century.

## The object

It is a fragment of a wooden disc, probably softwood, perhaps larch or spruce, although some sources say oak. The article gives 7 cm in width, 1 cm in thickness and a central hole 1.7 cm in diameter. There are triangular notches chip-carved on the rim. The disc has eight increments in each quadrant, 32 in all, which matches the traditional mariner's compass, and a small round dimple on the ninth increment marks east.

## The idea of the sun compass

Carl V. Sølver examined the notches and the scored lines and concluded it was a sun compass for finding true north. The central hole would take a handle with a horizontal directional pin and a vertical pin that casts a shadow. Sølver made a speculative illustration, a replica was built from it, and Vebæk ended up accepting the explanation. The gnomonic lines would be consistent with the summer solstice and the equinoxes, and in 1990 it was shown under the microscope that they were deliberately traced twice. In use, the disc is held level, the positions of the shadow are marked through the day, and the line of closest approach, at noon, indicates north.

## What was tested later

In 1984, the expedition of Ragnar Thorseth, on a replica of a Norse ship, tested replica discs against a modern compass on the passage between Iceland and Greenland, and the deviation was described as "negligible". In 2013, researchers at Eötvös University, in Hungary, proposed that the disc could also measure latitude. In 2018, another study from the same university concluded that sky-polarimetric navigation worked well at the spring equinox and the summer solstice, even with clouds. A similar object found near Wolin, in Poland, in 2000, strengthened the hypothesis.

## In short

The disc is a small fragment, and the sun compass hypothesis is the most accepted, but it is not a proof. Even so, it changed the idea that the Norse sailed only by eye.

## Terms

*Gnomon* is the rod that casts a shadow to mark the hour or the direction. *Equinox* is the day when night and day have the same length. *Sunstone* is a crystal, perhaps Iceland spar, that would help find the sun.

## What remains uncertain

The material, softwood or oak, is debated. The magnetic compass only appears in Europe in the early 13th century, and the article recalls that, with land out of sight, finding true north by day would be hard. Some scholars continue to argue against Norse navigation instruments. The use for measuring latitude and the twilight compass are proposals of researchers, not conclusions.
