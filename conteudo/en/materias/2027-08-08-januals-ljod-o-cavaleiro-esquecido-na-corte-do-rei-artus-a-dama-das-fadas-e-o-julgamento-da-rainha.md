The *Januals ljóð* is one of the twenty-one texts of the *Strengleikar*, the collection of Breton lais translated at the court of King Hákon Hákonarson of Norway. Its source is Marie de France's *Lanval*, one of the most widely read lais of the Middle Ages. The hero, called Janual in the Norse title, is a knight at the court of King Artús, and the story mixes a fairy adventure with a court intrigue.

## The knight without reward

Janual serves at King Artús's court, but he is a foreigner, and when the king hands out lands and gifts, he is left out. Without friends and without money, he rides out alone. By a river, two maidens lead him to a rich tent, where a lady of great beauty awaits him. She says she has come for love of him.

## The gifts and the condition

The lady promises him wealth and a purse that never empties. In return she sets a condition: Janual must not speak of her to anyone. If he does, he will lose her forever. She will come to him whenever he wishes, as long as he is alone. Janual returns to court rich, gives gifts, ransoms prisoners and helps the poor.

## The queen

The queen of the court proposes love to him. Janual refuses, out of loyalty to the king, and, pressed, lets slip that he loves a lady whose humblest maid surpasses the queen in beauty. Offended, she accuses Janual before the king of having insulted her. By speaking of the lady he broke the condition, and the lady no longer appears.

## The trial

The king summons Janual and puts him on trial before the barons of the court. Janual cannot prove what he said, and a sentence of death or exile seems certain. As the verdict is about to be given, two maidens arrive on horseback and announce the lady, then two more, and finally the lady herself, on a white palfrey. She proves the truth of his words and answers the queen. Her beauty wins over the verdict, and Janual is cleared. The lady leaves the court, and he mounts behind her toward the land of the fairies, and never returns.

## The Norse text

The *Januals ljóð* is part of the manuscript De la Gardie 4-7, of 1250 to 1270, in Uppsala. Like most of the *Strengleikar*, the translation follows the sense of the French closely, and Hákon's Norway thus received in prose one of the stories that would help shape the Norse image of King Artús and his court.
