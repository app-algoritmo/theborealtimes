Somerled (in Middle Irish *Somairle* or *Somhairle*, in Old Norse *Sumarliði*) was a 12th-century Norse-Gaelic lord who built a kingdom of Argyll and the Isles by marriage and conquest.

## Uncertain origins

Little is known of his youth. He probably had Norse-Gaelic ancestry and may have been born in northern Ireland. Wikipedia says his ancestry is "masked in obscurity and myth". His father is given as GilleBride and his grandfather as GilleAdamnan, names that appear to be corroborated in Irish annals. Pedigrees beyond two or three generations are unreliable.

## Marriage and rebellion

Around 1140, he married Ragnhild, daughter of Óláfr Guðrøðarson, king of Man and the Isles (died 1153). Through this marriage he later claimed the kingdom of the Crovan dynasty. In November 1153, the Chronicle of Holyrood says Somerled rose in rebellion, with his nephews, against the new king, Malcolm IV.

## Epiphany night

In 1156 (or 1157), Somerled backed the claim of his son Dugald to Man and the Isles and faced Godred Olafsson in a sea battle, with 80 ships, according to the account. The chronicle dates it to the night of Epiphany, and historians disagree whether it was January 1156 or 1157. The sources also differ on the place: off Islay or possibly off Man. The result was inconclusive, and the kingdom was divided. Around 1158, Somerled drove Godred out and ruled the whole kingdom until his death.

## Renfrew

In 1164, Somerled died at the battle of Renfrew, during a seaborne invasion of mainland Scotland, against forces of Herbert, bishop of Glasgow, and Baldwin of Biggar. The Chronicle of Mann gives 160 ships, but the article warns that such precise counts are doubtful.

## Descendants and genetics

According to the Chronicle of Mann, his sons were Dugald, Ranald, Angus and Olaf. Dugald's line produced the MacDougalls, and Ranald's produced the Lords of the Isles and Clan Donald. Genetic studies of 2004 and 2011 found a shared marker, from a subgroup of haplogroup R1a, rare among Celtic speakers and common in Norway, and point to a Scandinavian origin of the paternal line. Historians today see him acting within the same Norse-Gaelic culture as his rivals, and not as a Celtic hero who expelled the Vikings.
