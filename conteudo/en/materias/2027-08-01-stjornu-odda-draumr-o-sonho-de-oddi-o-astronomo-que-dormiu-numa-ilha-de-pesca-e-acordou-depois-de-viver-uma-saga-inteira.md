The *Stjörnu-Odda draumr*, "the dream of Stjörnu-Oddi", is an Icelandic tale that turns the idea of a saga upside down: here, whoever hears the story enters it. It reached us in a copy made by Árni Magnússon in 1686, the manuscript AM 555h 4to, which comes from the Vatnshyrna codex of the late fourteenth century, now lost.

## The man of the stars

Oddi Helgason, called Stjörnu-Oddi, "Oddi of the Stars", is a farmhand and sky observer who lived in the twelfth century. The tale describes him as skilled and honest, but not a poet. He works for Þórðr, at Múli, in Reykjardalr, in northern Iceland, and is sent to fish at Flatey. There, he sleeps.

## The dream within the dream

In a dream, Oddi is back at Múli, where a guest begins to tell a saga. King Hróðbjartr rules eastern Gautland, and his son Geirviðr is about eight when his father dies and is buried in a mound. The daughter of Jarl Hjörvarðr, Hléguðr, is violent and, threatened with banishment by her father, sets out with three ships and becomes a viking. As a boy of twelve, King Geirviðr decides to face two outlaws, Garpr and Gnýr, who terrorise the forest of Jöruskógr. The court poet, Dagfinnr, goes with him, stays on a hill as a lookout, and the king kills the two and finds a house full of treasure.

It is at this point that, when the name Dagfinnr is spoken, Oddi enters the story and begins to dream it as if he were Dagfinnr himself. The guest vanishes from the dream.

## The sea of Síldasund

Years later, Hléguðr returns and conquers Gautland. She sends word to Geirviðr that she wants half the kingdom or battle, and he chooses battle. In the strait of Síldasund, the fleets clash, and the king's side suffers many losses at first. Hléguðr uses sorcery to stay unseen, but Dagfinnr sees her at the prow of the king's ship in a monstrous form. Guided by him, Geirviðr beheads her. Her followers surrender.

The king is enthroned again on the mound, and Dagfinnr recites a longer poem. When the king offers to find him a wife, Dagfinnr chooses Hlaðreið, the king's sister, and the two marry.

## The waking

The saga ends, and Oddi wakes. He remembers the dream well and the verses he composed, although only part of the poem has survived. The narrator says the tale seems strange but can be trusted, since Oddi was held to be exact and truthful. And he excuses the clumsy poetry: it was made asleep.

## What remains uncertain

The text mixes a historical figure, Oddi, with a legendary saga of kings of Gautland, and this raises doubts about what the author knew and what he invented. The date of composition is not secure.
