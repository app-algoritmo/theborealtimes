At Jelling, in Jutland, the Jelling 1 stone says that Gorm the Old had the monument made for his wife, Þyri. The stories that came later tied her to a wall.

## What the stone says

According to the sources, Gorm had the stone made in memory of Þyri after her death. The inscription calls her "Danmarkar bót". The expression is translated in several ways: "Denmark's adornment", "strength", "salvation" or "remedy" (Imer, Åhfeldt and Zedig, 2023; Randsborg, 2008). The name "Danebod" comes from this expression, also cited in the Jómsvíkinga saga.

## The chroniclers

Saxo Grammaticus (twelfth century) makes her a daughter of Æthelred the Unready and describes her as serious and cunning: she is said to have demanded all of Denmark as her dowry and ordered the building of the Danevirke wall. Sven Aggesen tells another version: Þyri was of German origin, married to Gorm by arrangement of the emperor, refused to pay tribute and had the wall built partly to end it. Aggesen says she deceived the emperor into thinking the walls were against Gorm. The Historia Norwegiæ says that Gorm and Þyri had a daughter, Gunnhild, but this is not confirmed by other sources.

## What the archaeology of the wall says

The first phase of the Danevirke predates Þyri's reign: the trees used in it were felled in 737 AD, and the defences were extended until the twelfth century (Ramskou, 1963; Bregnsbo and Jensen, 2022). So the wall cannot have been the work of a single queen, although a later part may have been built in her time.

## What scholars debate

Imer et al. (2023) and Hollander (1928) argue that "Danmarkar bót" may refer to Gorm, not to Þyri, and that her prominence grew later, in the works of medieval chroniclers. The source says this is not the majority view. It is also debated whether seven runestones that mention her refer to the same woman, and whether the northern mound at Jelling is her grave: tree-ring dating of wooden objects gives 959–960 AD, but no body has been found (Whitbread, 1967; Randsborg, 2008).

## In short

What can be said of Þyri is little: she was Gorm's wife, and a stone calls her something like "Denmark's adornment". The wall-building queen is an image the chroniclers raised later.
