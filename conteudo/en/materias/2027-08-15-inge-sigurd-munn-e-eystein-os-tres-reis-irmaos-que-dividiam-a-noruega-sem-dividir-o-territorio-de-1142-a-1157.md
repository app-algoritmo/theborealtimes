After Harald Gille was killed, in 1136, Norway had several kings at the same time. Three of them were brothers, sons of Harald, and all reigned over the whole country, without dividing the territory. It is a strange arrangement for anyone thinking of divided kingdoms, and it is the key to understanding why the quarrel ended in blood.

## Inge, the boy crowned at one year old

Inge was born in 1135, son of Harald Gille and Ingiríðr Ragnvaldsdóttir, who remained influential throughout his reign. He was raised in Viken by Ögmund, or Ámund, Gyrðarson. He was named king at the Borgarting *thing*, near Sarpsborg, when he was one year old. He had a hump on his back and difficulty walking. *Heimskringla* and *Morkinskinna* explain this by the battle of 1137, in which a guardian carried him; Saxo Grammaticus says a maid dropped him as a baby. The nickname *krókhryggr*, "hunchback", does not appear in medieval sources. *Heimskringla* describes him as handsome, cheerful, generous and popular, with curly yellow hair.

## Sigurd Munn, the "Mouth"

Sigurd was born in 1133, son of Harald and Thora Guttormsdotter, and was raised in Trøndelag. His nickname, *Munn*, means "the Mouth". He was proclaimed king at the Eyra *thing* in 1136, and never married.

## Eystein, the one who came from Scotland

Eystein, born around 1125, was the son of Harald and Bjaðǫk, and seems to have been born in Scotland, where his father was raised. In 1142, several Norwegian *lendmenn* went to fetch him, and his mother came too. He began to reign with his half-brothers. In the early 1150s, according to *Heimskringla* and the Orkney saga, he raided Scotland, captured Harald Maddadson, earl of Orkney, in Caithness, and demanded a large ransom. He also attacked the coast at Aberdeen and plundered the English coast during the Anarchy. *Heimskringla* describes him as dark-complexioned, of medium height, prudent and capable, but says that avarice and narrowness cost him support.

## Bergen, 1155

In 1152, the Norwegian Church gained an independent archbishopric at Nidaros. In 1155, the three brothers met in Bergen to keep the peace, but Inge accused Sigurd and Eystein of planning to depose him. Sigurd denied it. A few days later, one of Inge's guards was killed by a man of Sigurd's. On the advice of his mother and of Gregorius Dagsson, Inge ordered an attack on the house where Sigurd was staying. Sigurd had few men and no mercy was shown. He was buried beside the old cathedral of Bergen, where the Bergenhus fortress stands today. Eystein arrived late, after his brother's death.

## Moster and Bohuslän

The sagas say that Sigurd and Eystein planned to strip Inge of his title, although modern historians see in this a justification for Inge's own aggression. In 1157, the two brothers' troops met near Moster, on the west coast. Inge had more men, and Eystein's deserted him. Eystein fled overland toward Viken, was left by his men and captured in the area of present-day Bohuslän. His captors killed him, on 21 August 1157. He was buried in the church at Foss, and *Heimskringla* says the locals began to venerate him as a saint.

## What remains

The sagas portray Eystein and Sigurd negatively and Inge as the rightful king. They rely in part on *Hryggjarstykki*, written between 1150 and 1170 and now lost. What was left was a country with a single king, Inge, and supporters of Sigurd and Eystein who soon rallied around Sigurd's son, Haakon.
