Heiðrún is a goat in Norse mythology who stands on Valhalla. The source consulted for this piece gathers the few lines the sources give her: what she eats, what runs from her, an insult in which her name appears and a hypothesis about the origin of that name. The article itself warns that the etymology is still debated and that it needs more citations, and this piece follows that caution.

## What the two Eddas say

In the *Poetic Edda*, Heiðrún is mentioned twice. In the *Grímnismál* she is described in a way similar to what Snorri Sturluson wrote later. The *Prose Edda* describes her in the *Gylfaginning*, chapters 38 to 43, according to the article. Because Snorri quotes other stanzas of the *Grímnismál*, the article considers it reasonable that he knew the stanza about Heiðrún and used it as a source.

## The leaves and the mead

Heiðrún is a nanny goat who stands on Valhalla. She eats the foliage of the tree Læraðr, which the article calls famous in the sources. Mead runs from her udders every day, and that mead fills a vessel. The amount is enough for all the einherjar to become fully drunk.

## The goat in an insult

In the poem *Hyndluljóð*, the giantess Hyndla uses the term "Heiðrún" as an insult against the goddess Freyja. Thorpe and some other translators render it as "she-goat". The article records the use but does not explain why that word would offend, and this piece does not go beyond what it states.

## The stag and the name

The heavenly stag Eikþyrnir is linked to Heiðrún's name. Anatoly Liberman proposes that *Heiðþyrnir*, the name of the lowest heaven in Scandinavian cosmology, formed from *heið*, "bright sky", was split into two halves. From those halves the names of the goat Heiðrún and of the stag Eikþyrnir would have arisen. The element *rún* may have concealed several puns, but it is also a common suffix in female names.

## Terms

*Heiðrún* is the name of the goat, and *Læraðr* is the tree she eats from. *Eikþyrnir* is the heavenly stag tied to her name. The *einherjar* are those who drink the mead in Valhalla. *Heið* means "bright sky", and *rún* is the final element of the name. The article also lists English forms of the name, among them Heidrun, Heidhrun, Heithrun and Heidrún, which are only ways of spelling the same name.

## What remains uncertain

The article says that the etymology of Heiðrún "remains debatable", and the same is true of the modern German name Heidrun. Liberman's hypothesis is a scholar's proposal, not a fact.
