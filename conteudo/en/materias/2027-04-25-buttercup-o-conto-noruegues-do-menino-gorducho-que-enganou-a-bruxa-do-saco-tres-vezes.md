The tales of Asbjørnsen and Moe have heroes of all kinds. This one has a chubby boy who loves sweets and is called Buttercup. The villain is an old hag who carries her head under her arm. And the tale has a dark humour, like many folktales.

## The boy and the dog

An old wife is baking. The dog Goldtooth barks, and the mother sends Buttercup out to see what it is. He comes back saying the hag is coming. His mother hides him under the kneading-trough.

The hag asks for Buttercup and says she has brought him a little silver knife. The boy pops out of his hiding place, and she persuades him to climb into her sack to fetch it. Then she carries him off on her back.

## The three journeys

Tired, the hag asks: "How far is it off to Snoring?" Buttercup answers "half a mile". She sets the sack down and sleeps in the wood. He cuts a hole in the sack, puts a great fir-tree root inside and runs home.

On the second day, she comes back with a silver spoon. This time the distance to Snoring is a mile and a half, and Buttercup puts a great stone in the sack. When the hag tries to boil him, the stone punches a hole in the pot, the water runs out and the fire goes out.

On the third day, she brings a silver fork. This time she takes him home, on a Sunday, and orders her daughter to kill and boil him while she goes to church to invite guests to dinner.

## The daughter and the chimney

Buttercup tells the girl he will show her how it is done: he tells her to lay her head on the chopping-block. Then he chops it off, puts her head in the bed and her body in the pot. He climbs onto the roof, drags up the root and the stone and leaves one over the door and the other at the top of the chimney.

The hag and her husband come back, take the head in the bed for the sleeping daughter and taste the broth. Buttercup calls from the chimney. When they come out to look, he drops the root and the stone, and their heads are broken. He takes the gold and silver he finds in the house, goes back to his mother and becomes rich.

## What the tale does

It is a tale of repetition in threes. Each day the hag turns up with a bigger silver gift, and each time the boy finds a weight to put in the sack: first a root, then a stone. The third day breaks the pattern, because the hag takes the boy home and he must use his wits on her territory. This is a harsh tale, which modern retellings for children usually soften.

## In short

Buttercup is carried off in a sack by the old hag three times and escapes twice by leaving a root and a stone in his place. The third time, he tricks the hag's daughter, takes the roof and drops the weights on the two of them. The text here comes from Dasent's translation.
