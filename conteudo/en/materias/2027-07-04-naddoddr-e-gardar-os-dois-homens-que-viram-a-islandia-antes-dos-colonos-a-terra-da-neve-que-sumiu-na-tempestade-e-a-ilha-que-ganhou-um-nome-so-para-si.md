According to the Landnámabók, the first Scandinavian to reach Iceland was Naddoddr, a Norwegian. He was bound for the Faroe Islands when a storm pushed him west, and he sighted a coast nobody knew. He climbed a hill and saw no smoke and no sign of people. When he left, it began to snow, and so the place became known as Snæland, "Snowland".

## The Swede from Zealand

Garðarr Svavarsson was a Swede who owned land on Zealand and had a wife from the Hebrides. In the 860s he was heading there to claim his father-in-law's inheritance when a storm in the Pentland Firth swept him north, to the east coast of Iceland. The Landnámabók in Haukr Erlendsson's version is the main source on him.

## Around the island

Garðarr was the first to sail all the way around the island, and so proved it was an island. He landed at Skjálfandi, built a house and wintered at the place now called Húsavík. When he went back he praised the land and called it Garðarshólmr, "Garðarr's island", after his own name.

## Who stayed

In the Landnámabók's version, when Garðarr left, a man called Náttfari stayed behind on the island with a male and a female slave. The place was named Náttfaravík. The Landnámabók presents them as the first permanent inhabitants. What remained of Garðarr was his son, Uni the Dane, who later emigrated to Iceland and tried to win it for the king of Norway, with himself as earl. He was killed.

## Three names

Snæland, Garðarshólmr and, later, Ísland: each visitor named the island after what he saw or what he wanted. The name that stuck was the third, given by Flóki Vilgerðarson after seeing a fjord full of ice.

## What the source does not settle

The account mixes a tradition that was only put on paper two centuries later with a common theme, that of the ship driven off course by a storm. Even so, the date (the 860s) and the short distance from the Faroes and the Hebrides make both accounts plausible.
