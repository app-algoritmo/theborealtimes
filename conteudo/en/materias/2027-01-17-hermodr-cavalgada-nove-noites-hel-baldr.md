Hermóðr is, in Norse mythology, a son of Odin and a brother of Baldr. The source consulted explains the name as something close to "war-spirit". His best-known role appears in the *Gylfaginning*, the part of Snorri Sturluson's *Prose Edda*, in chapter 49.

## The ride

After Baldr's death, the gods are too stunned to act. Frigg asks who will ride to Hel to ask for Baldr's return in exchange for a ransom. Hermóðr volunteers and mounts Sleipnir, Odin's horse.

He rides nine nights through dark valleys to the Gjöll bridge, covered in gold and guarded by the maiden Móðguðr. She tells him Baldr has already passed and sends him on downward and to the north. At the gate of Hel, Hermóðr tightens Sleipnir's girth, mounts again and makes the horse leap the gate.

## In Hel's house

In the hall, he sees Baldr in the seat of honour and asks Hel to free him, reminding her of the mourning of the Æsir. Hel answers that Baldr will be freed only if all things, living and dead, weep for him.

## The gifts

Baldr sends the ring Draupnir back to Odin. Nanna sends a linen robe for Frigg and a finger-ring for Fulla.

## Other texts with the same name

The article lists other mentions. In the *Hyndluljóð*, stanza 2, the name seems to refer to a mortal hero who receives a helmet and a mail-coat from the Highfather. In the *Hákonarmál*, stanza 14, Hermóðr and Bragi receive King Hákon the Good in Valhalla, without it being clear whether either is understood as a god. And in *Beowulf*, Heremod is an exiled Danish king who, in the Old English genealogies, descends from Sceafa and is usually the father of Scyld.

## Terms

*Sleipnir* is Odin's horse, which Hermóðr rides; *Gjöll* is the gold-covered bridge; *Móðguðr* is the maiden who guards it; *Draupnir* is the ring that Baldr sends back. The article does not deal with other sources, such as the *Skáldskaparmál* or the *Gesta Danorum*, and so this piece does not either. What it presents is the scene of the *Gylfaginning* and the mentions of similar names in three other texts.

## What remains uncertain

Most manuscripts call Hermóðr a son of Odin, but the Codex Regius, usually considered the best, calls him *sveinn Óðins*, which may mean "Odin's servant" rather than "son". A later passage calls him Baldr's brother, and a list of Odin's sons includes him, which favours the reading of son. The link between the Hermóðr of the *Edda* and the Heremod of *Beowulf* is only a parallel of names, and does not prove that they are the same figure.
