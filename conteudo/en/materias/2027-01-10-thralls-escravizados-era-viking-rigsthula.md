According to the source consulted, the *thrall* (in Old Norse, *þræll*) was an enslaved person or serf in Scandinavia in the Viking Age, below the *karl*, the freeman, and the *jarl*, the noble. The article cites the *Rígsþula* for this three-step order. The word comes from a Germanic root meaning "runner".

## How someone became a thrall

There were four paths. The first was inheritance: children of thralls were born thralls. The second was entering of one's own will, through poverty or debt, in a process called *flatföring*. The third was sentencing for a crime, for life or for a limited time. The fourth was capture in war: at first local, and later, with raids abroad, an important source.

## How many there were

Some estimates speak of up to thirty enslaved people per household, but most families had only one or two. The article gives no total for the region.

## The work

Men tended livestock, fished and worked in the forests. Women cooked and milked the cows, and a minority were concubines. Some thralls had special roles: the *bryte* governed a farm in place of an absent owner, and the *fostre* or *fostra*, raised among the owner's children, could marry free people, and the children of those unions were born free.

## Value before the law

The death of a freeman was compensated with a *weregild* of about 200 solidi. The death of an enslaved person was treated as a loss of property, compensated by the value of the worker. The article itself also says that thralls were given a *wergeld* as a penalty for unlawful killing, which sits badly with the explanation of property loss, and the text does not resolve that tension.

## Freedom

Erling Skjalgsson set each thrall a fixed value and ransom, and they could buy their freedom with a year or two of earnings. Owners could free them in a will or against payment. The freedman, the *leysingi*, still owed allegiance to his former owner and had to vote as he wished. Full freedom took at least two generations, and, if a freedman died without descendants, the former owner inherited his property.

## The end of slavery

Iceland's Gray Goose Laws allowed slavery until 1270, but the last mention is around 1122 to 1133. Denmark phased it out in the twelfth century. The *landslov* of Magnus Lagabøte, of 1274, in Norway, no longer recognised it. Sweden abolished it county by county, ending in 1335.

## What remains uncertain

The article gives no price in shillings for a thrall. The figures of thirty enslaved people per household are estimates, and the tension over the value of an enslaved person's life before the law, seen above, was left unanswered.
