The *Parcevals saga ok Valvens þáttr* is the Norse version of Chrétien de Troyes's *Perceval*, or *Le Conte du Graal*. It belongs to the group of chivalric sagas usually associated with King Hákon Hákonarson of Norway. According to Geraldine Barnes, *Erex saga* was probably written alongside *Ívens saga* and *Parcevals saga*, and *Parcevals saga* may also have been commissioned by the king, though that is only a hypothesis.

## The young Parceval

Chrétien's romance begins with a boy raised in the forest by his widowed mother, who keeps him away from chivalry. Parceval meets knights, decides to become one and goes to King Artús's court. He learns the craft, wins the lady he loves and, in a castle, sees a strange procession: a bleeding lance, a precious dish, a wounded king. He stays silent, without asking what it means. In the morning, the castle is empty.

## The question

That question not asked is the centre of the romance. In Chrétien, Parceval's silence will cost dearly, and he spends the rest of the work trying to understand what he lost. The Norse saga translates the story, but ends abruptly. According to scholarly analysis, Parceval never asks the question about the king's suffering, and yet lives happily. In Wolfram von Eschenbach, the question is essential.

## The Valvens þáttr

The *Valvens þáttr* is a separate narrative strand, centred on the adventures of Valven, the Gawain of Arthurian legend, which Chrétien interleaves with Parceval's. The saga presents them together. Like *Ívens saga*, it uses repeated structures to link passages similar in theme, and its author attempted end rhyme, although the text is in prose.

## An open debate

Date, authorship and link with the court remain under discussion. *Ívens saga* and *Parcevals saga* belong to Hallberg's so-called "Tristram group," but Blaisdell doubted the grouping, and it is not known whether the author was Norwegian or Icelandic. Studies treat the author as someone who made a work, not as a mere translator.

## Why it matters

Chrétien's *Perceval* was left unfinished. The Norse saga carries the story into another language and leaves it with an abrupt ending, preserving in Norse one of the most discussed texts of medieval literature.
