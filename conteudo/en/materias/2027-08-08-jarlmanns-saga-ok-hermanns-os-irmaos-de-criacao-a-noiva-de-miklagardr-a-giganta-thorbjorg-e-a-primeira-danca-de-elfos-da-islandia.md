The *Jarlmanns saga ok Hermanns*, also called *Hermanns saga ok Jarlmanns*, is an Icelandic chivalric saga. It survives in 76 manuscripts, by a 2020 count, more than almost any other Icelandic saga. Only *Mágus saga jarls* is comparable.

## The foster-brothers

Hermann, son of the king of France, and Jarlmann, son of an earl, are foster-brothers of the same age. Hermann sends Jarlmann to Miklagarðr, the Constantinople of the Norse texts, to woo Ríkilát, who has turned down many suitors. Jarlmann wins her for Hermann with a magic ring, but she can leave only when the forces of another suitor have been driven off.

## The abduction and the giantess

At the wedding, the old king Rudent of Serkland abducts Ríkilát and imprisons her, intending to marry her. Jarlmann pretends to love Þorbjörg, the giantess who guards Ríkilát, and the weddings are double: Rudent with Ríkilát, Jarlmann with Þorbjörg. Hermann kills Rudent and recovers his bride. Jarlmann kills Þorbjörg in the bridal bed.

## Back in France

The group returns to France, where there is a second double wedding. Jarlmann marries Herborg, the king's sister, and receives half the kingdom.

## Sources and influences

According to scholars, the saga is related to *Konráðs saga*, which the shorter version cites explicitly, and which also treats the loyal companion who is unjustly suspected. From *Tristrams saga ok Ísöndar* seem to come the proxy wooing, the bride as healer and the proxy wooer who becomes a lover. The scene in which Jarlmann draws a portrait of Hermann for Ríkilát was probably inspired by the hall of statues in Tristram's saga.

## Two versions and a dance

The saga exists in two medieval versions: an "older" one, longer and more learned, first attested in the *Eggertsbók* (AM 556a-b 4to), and a "younger" one, shorter and livelier. Recent scholarship considers both independent adaptations of a lost original, not one derived from the other. The earliest manuscripts date from the later fifteenth century, and copies range from about 1660 to 1912.

It contains the first written evidence of the Icelandic *hringbrot* dance, a circle dance, and the first Icelandic attestation of elves dancing.
