The Icelandic sagas usually speak of men who founded families and fought over land. There are few women among the great settlers. Aud the Deep-Minded, in Icelandic *Auðr djúpúðga*, is one of them. Her story appears in the *Landnámabók*, the "Book of Settlements", and in the *Laxdæla saga*, the "Saga of the People of Laxárdalr".

## Who Aud was

According to the sources, Aud was the daughter of Ketill Flatnose, a Norwegian chieftain, and of Yngvid, daughter of Ketill. She married Olaf the White, who ruled Dublin as king. The couple had a son, Thorstein the Red, who conquered parts of northern Scotland. But Thorstein died in battle, and Aud, by then a widow, saw that her family was in danger in the British Isles.

## The ship in secret

The decision that made her known was this: she had a *knarr*, a sturdy cargo ship suited to long crossings, built in secret in a forest. When the ship was ready, she boarded with her family and many people. Before leaving for Iceland, the route passed through the Orkney Islands, where one of her granddaughters, Groa, was married. The crossing, according to the tradition, was successful.

## The land and the freed men

Aud arrived at Breiðafjörður, in western Iceland, and claimed a wide stretch of land, in the region of Dalasýsla, between the rivers Dagverðará and Skraumuhlaupsá. According to the sources, she gave land to many who had travelled with her. Among those who received a share were freed men, who had been prisoners on board, such as Vífill, who was given a valley called Vífilsdalur. That gesture, for a settler, earned her the respect of descendants.

## The Christian faith

The sources say Aud was a Christian, baptised, and she is credited with bringing Christianity to Iceland, even before the official conversion in the year 1000. She is said to have raised crosses on the hill of Krosshólar, where she said her prayers. Her descendants, according to the tradition, returned to paganism, and the place came to be regarded as sacred for other reasons.

## What the sources are

It is important to remember that the *Landnámabók* and the *Laxdæla saga* were written centuries after the events, between the 12th and 13th centuries. They mix family records with stories, and the details of Aud's life cannot be confirmed by other testimony. Aud also appears, in passing, in *Njáls saga*, *Eyrbyggja saga*, *Eiríks saga rauða* and *Grettis saga*, which shows she was remembered in many families.

## What remains

Aud symbolises the Iceland of the first settlers, a land where someone could arrive, choose a valley and found a lineage. Her epithet, "the deep-minded", suggests that the tradition remembered her as a woman who planned well.
