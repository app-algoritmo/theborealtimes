*Vættir*, singular *vættr*, means "being" or "creature", and is a relative of Old English *wiht*. The compound *landvættir* has been rendered as "guardian spirits of a country", "land-spirits" or "nature spirits". The Wikipedia article consulted gathers four episodes from the sources.

## The law against figureheads

An early law, the *Úlfljótslǫg*, prohibited sailing to the coast with carved figureheads on the prow, because they could frighten the landvættir.

## The Landnámabók

After Hjörleifr is killed by his thralls at Hjörleifshöfði, the text says no one dared settle where he had lived, because of the landvættir. It also describes a *bergbúi*, "rock-dweller", who gives Björn prosperous goats, and offerings to a waterfall and a holy grove.

## The sorcerer in whale form

In the *Heimskringla*, in the saga of Óláfr Tryggvason, King Harald Bluetooth sends a sorcerer, in whale form, to spy on Iceland. He finds the mountains and hills "full of landvættir" and is driven off from each side by a different being: a dragon and serpents in the east, a great bird in the north, a bull with beasts in the west and a giant with an iron staff in the south. The *Knýtlinga saga* calls these beings *óvættir*, "evil spirits".

## Egill's curse

In the *Egils saga*, in Norway, Egill raises a *níðstöng*, a horse head on a pole, and curses the landvættir so that they cannot find their dwellings until Eric Bloodaxe and Gunnhild leave Norway. They leave the following year, but the saga leaves the cause unclear.

## Terms

*Vættir* is the plural of *vættr*, "being"; *bergbúi* is the "rock-dweller"; *níðstöng* is the horse head on a pole, used for cursing; *óvættir* are the "evil spirits" of the *Knýtlinga saga*; *landdísir* are beings the article cites alongside elves and dwarfs.

## Four kinds of source

The episodes come from four very different kinds of text: an early law, a settlement book, a king's saga and a family saga. Each speaks of the landvættir with a distinct function: the law prohibits a gesture, the settlement book explains why a place was not occupied, the king's saga shows a defence of the island, and Egill's saga shows a curse.

## An island on four sides

The article arranges the scene from the *Heimskringla* by the points of the compass: east, north, west and south. A being corresponds to each. Scholars see in this arrangement, as the article tells it, a possible influence of the image of the four evangelists.

## What remains uncertain

It is unclear whether all the beings in the *Heimskringla* episode were considered landvættir. Scholars often think the four figures reflect the Christian image of the four evangelists. The nature of the landvættir is also debated: some see them as chthonic spirits or spirits of the dead, others as nature spirits. Their relation to elves, dwarfs and *landdísir* is debated, and the landvættir are not mentioned in the Eddic material. The Gulating laws of Norway, from the late 13th century, outlawed belief in them as heresy, so the evidence comes from sources shaped by Christianity.
