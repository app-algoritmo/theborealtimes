Dwarfs come from so many places in the Germanic imagination that they are hard to sum up. The source consulted deals with the Norse *dvergar* and their relatives in German and English, and shows that what is now thought of them, small, bearded and carrying a pickaxe, is only part of the story.

## The name

The English word *dwarf* comes from Old English *dweorg*, with relatives in Old Norse *dvergr* and Old High German *twerg*. The Proto-Germanic form is usually reconstructed as *dwergaz*, but the ultimate origin is much debated: there are proposals tied to "damage" and "deception", and the linguist Guus Kroonen suggests a verb meaning "to squeeze". The plural *dwarves* was popularised by Tolkien, who called it "private bad grammar".

## Where they came from

In the Prose Edda, dwarfs form like maggots in the flesh of Ymir, which becomes the earth. The Codex Regius version of the *Völuspá* says something else: that they were produced out of the earth. Four of them, Norðri, Suðri, Austri and Vestri, hold up the corners of the sky, made from Ymir's skull. The article recalls that the sky may have been imagined close to the horizon, and that does not make the dwarfs tall.

## Smiths of the gods

Sindri and Brokkr, brothers, forged Draupnir, Gullinbursti and Mjölnir. The sons of Ívaldi made Gungnir, Skíðblaðnir and Sif's hair. Dwarfs also made the chain Gleipnir and, in the *Sörla þáttr*, Freyja's necklace Brísingamen. Fjalarr and Galarr killed Kvasir and made the mead of poetry from his blood. Litr was kicked by Thor into Baldr's pyre. And Andvari, who lived as a pike, was forced by Loki to hand over his treasure, including the ring Andvaranaut.

## The Dvergatal list

A passage of the *Völuspá*, the Dvergatal, is a catalogue of names. It includes Álfr, "elf", Gandálfr and Vindálfr, which shows the overlap between dwarfs and elves, and even Yngvi, a name of the god Freyr. Every dwarf named in the list is male.

## Stone and sun

The *Völuspá* calls dwarfs "masters of the rocks", and some poetic names for stone speak of a dwarf's house. In the *Alvíssmál*, the dwarf Alvíss wants to marry Þrúðr, Thor's daughter, and Thor keeps him talking until dawn, when the sun turns him to stone. In the *Ynglinga saga*, a dwarf who fears the sun lures King Sveigðir into a stone, which closes behind him.

## In Germany and in illness

In German works, dwarfs often wear the Tarnkappe, a cloak of invisibility, and are great smiths. Alberich guards the Nibelung treasure and is defeated by Siegfried, and Dietrich von Bern fights the dwarf king Laurin. In Old English, *dweorg* could also mean "fever", and medical charms describe a dwarf-sickness that rides the patient. An eighth-century skull fragment from Ribe seems to invoke Odin against harmful dwarfs, and in Norwegian *dvergskott*, "dwarf-shot", is an animal disease.

## What remains uncertain

The origin of the word is not known, nor whether dwarfs began as nature spirits or as beings tied to death. It is also not known whether *svartálfar* and *dökkálfar* are the same as dwarfs, since they appear only in the Prose Edda. The early sources do not state their size, and names like Fullangr and Hár are ambiguous. Females appear only in late sagas, and there is no proof that dwarfs received offerings.
