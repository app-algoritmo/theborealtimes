Today we tend to find it natural that a country has one law for everyone. In medieval Norway, it was not so. Each region had its own assembly, its own *thing*, and each followed its own rules. In 1274, King Magnus VI Håkonsson, who reigned from 1257 to 1280, gave the kingdom a single code, the *Landslov*, the "law of the land". People came to call him Lagabøte, which means "the lawmender".

## Four laws in one

Before him, four jurisdictions kept their own laws, those of the *things* of Frostating, Gulating, Eidsivating and Borgarting. The *Landslov* was written to apply across the whole kingdom. In practice it existed in four books, one for each jurisdiction, with content largely similar and based on the earlier laws. Between 1274 and 1276, the text was taken to the regional assemblies.

According to the Norwegian Parliament, the code dealt with matters such as poor relief, the renting of land, mortgages and credit, sales and the shipping of goods. It abolished the right to avenge serious offences, such as injury, theft and defamation. It gave women a right of inheritance, equal to half of their brothers' share, and set the legal adulthood of women at 20. In 1276 came a separate law for the towns, the *Bylov*.

## The king, the Church and Iceland

Not everything was easy. The archbishop of Nidaros, Jon Raude, opposed the king revising church law. The dispute ended with the *Sættargjerden* of Tønsberg, in 1277, which gave the clergy tax relief and more legal privileges. In Iceland, the sister code, the *Jónsbók*, was sent by ship in the spring of 1280. Magnus died on 9 May of that year.

## A book for daily use

The *Landslov* was the most widely used non-religious book in medieval Norway. At least 41 copies or manuscripts survive, which gives an estimate of one manuscript for every 1,150 inhabitants, and each one required more than forty calfskins. The copies vary, and readers noted the differences in the margins, which shows that the text was adapted to each case, not followed to the letter.

According to the Parliament, it was the first European national code that was actually used. Parts of it remained in force for more than four hundred years. In 1604 it was revised and translated into Danish, in the *Norske Lov* of Christian IV, and in 1687 it was repealed, replaced by the *Norske Lov* of Christian V. In 2024, Norway celebrated the law's 750th anniversary.
