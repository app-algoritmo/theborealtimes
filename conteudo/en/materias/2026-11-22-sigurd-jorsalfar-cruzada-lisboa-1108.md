In 1108, according to the sagas, a Norwegian fleet passed along the coast of the Iberian Peninsula, and its crews attacked settlements near Lisbon. The leader of the expedition was Sigurd, son of King Magnus Barefoot, who became known as Jórsalafari, "the one who went to Jerusalem". What we know of the journey comes from texts written more than a century later, and so every detail calls for caution.

## The journey according to the sagas

Sigurd was about seventeen when he left Norway, around 1107, with a fleet that the *Heimskringla* counts at sixty ships. He reigned together with his brother Eystein, and the text shows that, on leaving, he entrusted the kingdom to him. The saga says that the group spent the winter in England and then went on to the region of Galicia, where it spent another winter, under an arrangement of coexistence with the local lord.

Next, the fleet passed along the western coast. The saga mentions attacks near Sintra and Lisbon, which it describes as a city half Christian, half Muslim, and it mentions Alcácer do Sal. At that time the kingdom of Portugal did not yet exist, and the region was under Almoravid rule. The Norsemen acted as crusaders and also as raiders, and the sagas take pride in the booty.

## The Mediterranean and Jerusalem

The fleet went on through the Mediterranean, and the saga names the islands of Formentera, Ibiza and Minorca, as well as a stop in Sicily. In Jerusalem, according to the text, King Baldwin I received Sigurd with great honours and showed him holy places, among them a relic of the Cross. The Norsemen are said to have helped take the city of Sidon, in 1110. Accounts by crusaders in the East confirm that a Norwegian fleet was on the coast, which supports the eastern part of the story.

The saga says that Sigurd then went to Constantinople, where Emperor Alexios I gave him a reception, and that he left his ships there. The king returned overland, crossing central Europe and Denmark, and reached Norway around 1111.

## What historians discuss

Historians point out that the Iberian part of the journey appears only in the Scandinavian sagas, written much later, and that there is no Iberian chronicle of the time that confirms it. Some think the details were embellished to make Sigurd a king worthy of the crusaders. Even so, the whole is plausible, because other Scandinavian groups made similar journeys, and the maritime routes of the Atlantic were known.

It is the first time that a Scandinavian king made a journey of this kind, and the saga uses it to show the kingdom of Norway as part of the Christian world of Europe.
