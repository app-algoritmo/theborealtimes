Borgund stave church is in Lærdal, in Vestland county, Norway. It was a Catholic parish church and later one of the Church of Norway, and today it is a museum, run by the Society for the Preservation of Ancient Norwegian Monuments.

## The date

It was built around 1200. The article gives a range of about 1180 to 1250, with later additions and restorations. The chancel and apse are from the 14th century. The source does not say how the date was established.

## How it is made

It is built of vertical wooden staves, hence the name, with tongue-and-groove walls. The plan has three naves, of the Sogn type, and the exterior is darkened by tar distilled from pine. Inside, twelve free-standing columns support the raised central nave.

## Dragons and carvings

Four dragon heads, on the gables, swoop from the roof ridges. Their function is uncertain: they may serve to ward off evil, rather than represent it. The article warns that the present heads may be 18th-century replacements. The west portal has dragons biting each other, and the ridge caps are openwork carvings of plants. Borgund is one of the few churches of this type to keep its ridge caps.

## The bell tower

To the south stands the only free-standing stave-built bell tower that survives in Norway, probably from the mid-13th century. It covers the remains of a medieval foundry where the church's bell was cast.

## Runes and legend

There is medieval runic graffiti, such as the common "Ave Maria". Another inscription, by a man named Þórir, blames the pagan Norns for his troubles and is dated to St Olaf's Mass. Reindeer antlers hang on the pillars, and a local story says they come from a reindeer shot during Mass. The article gives this as folklore.

## What saved it

The church stopped being used for worship in 1868, when a new church was built nearby. It became a museum and escaped the demolition that took many others of the time. The Society bought it in 1877, and a restoration in the early 1870s removed later additions, such as a gallery. It served as a model for Fantoft church in Bergen (1883), for the one in Hahnenklee, Germany (1908), and for four replicas in the United States.
