Many people have woken up feeling a weight on the chest, unable to move or cry out. In Nordic tradition, that weight had a name: *mara*. The word survives in the way several languages name the bad dream.

## A king and a mara

The oldest and best-known mention appears in the *Ynglinga saga*, part of the *Heimskringla*, written by the Icelander Snorri Sturluson around 1230. The saga tells the story of Vanlandi, king of the Swedes. According to it, Vanlandi visited the region the saga calls *Finnland* (a name that does not always match modern Finland), married Drífa, daughter of Snær, and left promising to return in three years. He stayed ten.

Drífa then sent for the witch Huld. The saga says the witch cast a spell, and that when Vanlandi wanted to sleep, a mara trod on him. He cried out that she was treading on him. His men held his head, and the mara trod on his legs. They held his legs, and she trod on his head, and the king died. It is a tradition preserved in writing centuries after it was told, and the saga is not a record of facts.

## A figure of folklore

In Scandinavian folklore collected in the 19th and 20th centuries, the mara appears as a being that sits on the chests of sleepers and suffocates them. In many accounts, she is the spirit of a living person, usually a woman, who leaves the body during the night. In others, she is a separate creature. People also blamed the mara for the knots that appeared in the mornings in the manes of horses in the stable.

## The word that remained

The English *nightmare* comes from *night* and the Old English *mære*, the same being. In Norwegian, the nightmare is *mareritt*, literally “the mara's ride”, and in Danish, *mareridt*. In Swedish, it is *mardröm*, “mara dream”. Similar words exist in German and French.

## What science says

Doctors today recognise a phenomenon called sleep paralysis. It occurs at the edge between sleeping and waking, when a person is conscious but cannot move, and often feels a presence or a weight on the chest. The folklorist David Hufford showed in 1982 that accounts of this kind appear in very different cultures, with local explanations. The mara is one of them.

Explaining a belief does not devalue it. It shows how the peoples of the North gave a name to what they experienced at night.

## Relatives in other languages

The theme is old and widespread. The French *cauchemar* joins the old verb *caucher*, “to tread”, to the same term *mare*, and the image of something that treads on a sleeper is the same as in the *Ynglinga saga*. Old German had *Mahr*, and Old English *mære*. The forms vary, but the idea of a being that rides or treads on sleepers recurs all over Europe.
