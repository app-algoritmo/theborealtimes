The *Geitarlauf* is one of the twenty-one texts of the *Strengleikar*, the collection of Breton lais rendered into Norse at the court of King Hákon Hákonarson. Its original is Marie de France's *Chevrefoil*, a short lai set within the legend of Tristan and Iseult, the same legend Brother Robert had translated in 1226 as the *Tristrams saga ok Ísöndar*. The title is a literal translation: *geitarlauf* means "goat's leaf," like the French *chèvrefeuille*, the honeysuckle.

## The exile and the meeting

Tristram has been banished from the court of the king of Cornwall, his uncle, because of his love for Ísönd, the queen. He came back to the region in secret and waited in the forest, by the road along which the queen's retinue was to pass on the way to a great feast. The queen, for her part, knew he was nearby.

## The hazel

To warn her, Tristram cut a hazel branch, carved his name on it and left it at the roadside. The sign had been agreed between the two: on seeing the branch, the queen would know he was there. As the retinue draws near, she sees the branch and recognises the message.

## The honeysuckle

What the carved name said, according to the poem, was an image: the two were like the hazel and the honeysuckle. When the vine winds around the trunk, the two live together, but if anyone tries to part them, the hazel dies quickly and the honeysuckle with it. "So it is with us," says the message: without you I cannot live, and without me, neither can you.

## The meeting

The queen has the retinue stop, steps aside with her trusted maid and goes into the wood. She finds Tristram, they talk, he tells her how he lives, and the two speak of the hope that the king will call him back. The meeting is brief, and they part in pain. Afterward Tristram composes a lai about what happened. Marie says the English call it *Gotelef* and the French *Chevrefoil*, and the Norse lai keeps the name for its meaning: *Geitarlauf*.

## The place of the text

The *Geitarlauf* is in the manuscript De la Gardie 4-7, of 1250 to 1270, in Uppsala. Like most of the *Strengleikar*, the translation follows the sense of the French closely. It also shows a familiar name, that of Tristram, in another form, more intimate than that of Robert's saga.
