In 1013 a Danish king achieved what many before him had tried and not reached: to be recognised as king of England. Sweyn, called Forkbeard, had ruled Denmark for almost thirty years and knew the English defences well, because he himself or his men had attacked the coast several times. This time, however, the aim was to take the crown. Within a few months he reached it, but he died soon after.

## Before the conquest

Since the end of the 10th century, attacks from Scandinavia had again struck England. The English king, Æthelred, tried various answers. He paid large sums in silver, the so-called *Danegeld*, to chiefs who led the fleets, and in 1002 he ordered the killing of Danes living in England, in an episode known as the St Brice's Day massacre, whose real scale is still debated. The tradition of several sources links this order to a desire for revenge on Sweyn's part, but that link is less certain than it seems.

In 1013 Sweyn gathered a fleet and set out from Denmark with his son Cnut. The *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* says that he arrived at Sandwich, in the south, in summer, and then sailed north along the coast to the mouth of the Humber, and went up the Trent to Gainsborough.

## A campaign through the north

The choice of the north was clever. The region of the so-called Danelaw had a population partly of Scandinavian origin, and the chronicler says the inhabitants submitted to Sweyn as soon as he arrived, and gave him hostages. From there he left part of the fleet and his son with the ships, and went south with the army. The towns of Oxford and Winchester surrendered next, and the king was accepted in other places where there was little will to resist.

London, however, resisted. The city had walls and a bridge over the Thames, and was defended by King Æthelred and by Thorkell the Tall, a Norse chief in the English king's service. Sweyn tried to take it and failed. He went, then, to the west, where the nobles of Wessex submitted. After that, London itself gave in, according to the chronicle, because the citizens feared the consequences of continuing against a king who seemed to have won.

## The end of Æthelred and the death of the conqueror

At the end of 1013, Æthelred fled England for Normandy, where his wife, Emma, and his children already were. Sweyn was accepted as king by a good part of the English, although he was not crowned. But he died on 3 February 1014, at Gainsborough, a few weeks later. The cause is unknown, and a later tradition, without a good basis, speaks of a miracle attributed to a saint.

The death of Sweyn changed everything again. The Danes in England proclaimed Cnut king, but the English nobles asked for the return of Æthelred, who came back. Only in 1016 did Cnut manage to establish himself, after a hard campaign.

## What historians say

The conquest of 1013 was too quick to be explained by force alone. Many researchers point to the exhaustion of the kingdom, the years of attacks, the taxes and the lack of trust between the king and his nobles. The submission of the north and of the west shows that many local groups preferred to accommodate the winner rather than go on fighting for a king who did not protect them well.

The sources have limits. The *Chronicle* was written by English authors, and there is no equivalent account from the Danish side, so Sweyn's point of view is seen only from outside.
