The Galloway Hoard is a treasure found in south-west Scotland. The source consulted tells how it was discovered, what it contained and what is still discussed.

## The find

In September 2014, Derek McLennan, a metal detectorist from Ayrshire, found the first silver object, an arm ring, on Church of Scotland land near Balmaghie, in Kirkcudbrightshire. The county archaeologist, Andrew Nicholson, excavated the site, and McLennan found a second, lower level of the hoard.

## The date

The article says the hoard was probably buried in the mid-ninth or tenth century.

## What there was

There was a silver vessel with other objects inside, wrapped in cloth and with its lid in place. Among the finds are silver arm rings, some with Anglo-Saxon runic inscriptions, Anglo-Saxon disc brooches and an Irish silver brooch, a silver pectoral cross with a wire chain, silver ingots, glass beads and a gold-encased touchstone. There are also gold objects, a gold ingot, rock crystal pieces and Byzantine silk.

## The vessel

At first the vessel was thought to be Carolingian, but later research showed it was made in Western Asia, probably in the Sasanid Empire. Its ornamentation includes a Zoroastrian fire altar, and lead isotope analysis links its silver and niello to the Nakhlak mine in Iran.

## The purchase

After a fundraising campaign in 2017, the National Museum of Scotland acquired the hoard. The total value was set at £1.98 million by an advisory panel, and the museum paid that sum. The finder and the General Trustees of the Church of Scotland shared the money.

## Terms

*Detectorist* is someone who searches for buried metal objects with a detector; *niello* is the dark inlay used to decorate silver; *Sasanid* refers to the Persian empire that the article points to as the probable origin of the vessel; a *touchstone* is a stone used to test the purity of metals. The article also speaks of *glebeland*, land belonging to a church.

## Two levels

The first object, a silver arm ring, was the first to appear. After the county archaeologist's excavation, McLennan found a second, lower level. The article records that it is not known why the hoard was buried in two levels.

## What the find shows

The set gathers pieces of very different origins: arm rings with Anglo-Saxon runic inscriptions, an Irish brooch, Byzantine silk and a vessel from Western Asia. This shows that objects from distant places reached Scotland and were kept together, but the article does not explain through whose hands they passed.

## What remains uncertain

The article lists doubts: who buried the hoard, and whether they were Vikings or Anglo-Saxons; why it was buried, and why in two levels; and whether it relates to a timber building and an early ecclesiastical site nearby. One suggestion cited is that it was "a safety deposit box that was never claimed".
