Iona is a small island of the Inner Hebrides, off the west coast of Scotland, about five kilometres long. In 563 the Irish monk Columba (in Irish, Colm Cille) founded a monastery there, which became one of the most important centres of Christianity in the Celtic world. More than two centuries later the place was known for being rich in books, crosses and objects of worship, and it lay too close to the sea.

## 795: the first warning

The Annals of Ulster record, for the year 795, an attack by foreigners on islands of the western coast. Researchers usually link this entry to the first Viking visit to Iona. The annals give no details, but the year marks the beginning of a long run of attacks on monasteries on islands and coasts.

## 806: Martyrs' Bay

In 806 the annals say that 68 members of the community were killed by the Vikings. Local tradition places the deaths at Martyrs' Bay, on the island's coast, and the name survives today. The annals' text is short; the rest is Iona's own memory.

## 825: Blathmac

In 825 the monk Blathmac was killed by the Vikings for refusing to reveal where Columba's relics were, hidden in the saint's tomb. We know this from a 9th-century poem by Walahfrid Strabo, of the monastery of Reichenau, in what is now southern Germany. So the story is a late and distant source, to be read with care.

## The book that fled to Kells

Around 807, after the raid of 806, the community began to move part of its activities to Ireland, where it founded the abbey of Kells, in County Meath. One tradition says that the Book of Kells, the famous illuminated gospel book, was begun in Iona and finished in Kells. Specialists debate where each part was copied, and there is no consensus. In 849, according to the annals, Columba's relics were divided between Ireland and Scotland.
