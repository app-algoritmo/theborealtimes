Surtr is, according to the source consulted, a *jötunn*, a giant, and the guardian of Muspelheim, one of the two realms said to exist before time, alongside Niflheim. The name is Old Norse for "black", or more narrowly "swart".

## The sources

He appears in the *Völuspá*, the *Vafþrúðnismál*, the *Fáfnismál* and the *Fjölsvinnsmál*, which are poems of the *Poetic Edda*, and in the *Gylfaginning* and the *Skáldskaparmál* by Snorri Sturluson, of the thirteenth century.

## Ragnarök

The *Völuspá* says Surtr comes from the south with flames and a very bright sword. The *Gylfaginning* puts him at the head of the "sons of Muspell", who cross the Bifröst bridge to the field Vígríðr. The *Vafþrúðnismál* describes that field as "the ordained field", a hundred leagues in each direction. Surtr fights Freyr, called "Beli's bane". According to the *Gylfaginning*, Freyr falls because he no longer has the sword he gave to Skírnir. Afterwards, Surtr sets the earth on fire. Líf and Lífthrasir survive hidden in Hoddmímis holt. In the sources, then, a world ends, not everything.

## Minor details

The *Fáfnismál* gives the island of the battle the name Óskópnir. The *Fjölsvinnsmál* may name Sinmara as Surtr's wife, but the line is obscure and the reading depends on an emendation of the text. The *Skáldskaparmál*, in an account that reduces the gods to men, links "Surt's fire" to the burning of Troy.

## Places with his name

Surtshellir is a volcanic cave in western Iceland that bears his name, and the *Landnámabók* describes a journey singing poems to a giant who was said to live there. Excavations between 2001 and 2013 found signs of ritual activity, not of outlaws, from 65 to 100 years before Iceland's conversion, around the year 1000, which may suggest a cult. Surtsey, the volcanic island that appeared in 1963 off Iceland, was also named after him. The character also appears in Marvel comics and in the film *Thor: Ragnarok*, of 2017, modern versions that do not come from the medieval sources.

## What scholars debate

Rudolf Simek thinks the concept is old and links the southern origin to Surtr's tie to fire and heat. Phillpotts saw in him an inspiration from Icelandic eruptions, perhaps a volcano demon. Orchard suggests that the *Gylfaginning* description may echo the angel with the flaming sword guarding Eden. Lindow notes that the name may suggest a charred appearance.

## What remains uncertain

None of these readings is definitive, and the article does not treat the link to volcanoes as fact. Each source adds a piece: the *Völuspá* gives the fire and the sword, the *Gylfaginning* the army and the bridge, the *Vafþrúðnismál* the field. What they support, together, is simpler: a guardian of fire, from the south, who in the end burns the world.
