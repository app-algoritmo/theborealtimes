The word *dís*, in Old Norse, means "lady", and the plural is *dísir*. According to the Wikipedia article consulted, it can denote a goddess, a ghost or a spirit tied to fate, benevolent or hostile. Researchers suggest the basic meaning is "goddess", but the etymology, tied to an Indo-European root of "to suck, suckle", is only a proposal.

## Where they appear

In the *Guðrúnarkviða I*, the valkyries are called "Herjan's dísir", that is, Odin's. In the *Atlamál*, stanza 28 says the dísir are dead women, and Glaumvör's dream about them survives only in part. In the *Hamðismál*, Sörli blames the dísir for having led him to kill Erpr, and in the *Grímnismál*, Odin says the wrath of the dísir killed a king: in both, the term stands in for norns. In the *Reginsmál*, an unmarried woman is insulted as a "dís ulfhuguð", and the dísir accompany a warrior to see him die in battle, like valkyries. In the *Krákumál*, Ragnarr says "the dísir invite me home".

The First Merseburg Charm, the only pagan German text of the kind, speaks of the *Idisi*, women who work battle magic. Snorri never mentions the dísir in the Prose Edda, but lists *Vanadís*, a name of Freyja, and *öndurdís*, a name of Skaði. In the *Þiðranda þáttr*, dísir in black kill Þiðrandi, and those in white fail to save him. In the *Hervarar saga*, a king's daughter holds an autumn dísablót, and Helga hangs herself in the *dísarsalr*.

## The dísablót

In western Scandinavia, it seems to have been a private family observance. At Gamla Uppsala, according to Snorri's *Heimskringla*, it took place in the month of Gói, between late February and early March, with an assembly and a fair that lasted about a week. The purpose was peace and the king's victory. Snorri visited Sweden in 1219 and says the chief sacrifice in Svíþjóð was at Uppsala.

In the *Ynglinga saga*, King Aðils dies at a dísablót: his horse stumbles while he rides around the *dísarsalr*, the goddess's hall, and he dies from the fall. The article suggests it may have been a ritual death, but that is only a possibility. The *Víga-Glúms saga* speaks of a dísablót at the start of winter, among family and friends, and the *Egils saga* is also cited.

## Today's Disting

After Christianisation, the assembly and fair moved to Candlemas, on 2 February. The name *Dísaþing*, now Disting, survived, and the Uppsala fair still takes place on the first Tuesday of February. It may be one of the oldest in Sweden.

## Dísir, norns, valkyries and fylgjur

Some scholars think *dísir* may be the original term and "valkyrie" a kenning for it. In two poems, dísir and norns blur together. In the *Þiðranda þáttr*, the character Thorhall identifies the dísir as *fylgjur*, protective family spirits.

## What remains uncertain

It is not known whether the dísir were fertility goddesses, clan guardians, war figures or spirits of the dead. Rudolf Simek sees them as varied manifestations of female goddesses. The *dísarsalr* is also poorly understood, as is the purpose of the Uppsala sacrifice. The *Hervarar saga* suggests the rite was performed by women, and the *Ynglinga saga*, that the king was the priest. And the article records that Gabriel Turville-Petre associated it with the Anglo-Saxon *modranect*, the "mothers' night", without saying whether this is accepted.
