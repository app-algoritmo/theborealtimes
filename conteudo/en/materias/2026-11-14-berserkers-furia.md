Berserker is the word the Norse sagas use for a kind of warrior who went into combat in a state of fury, without armour, howling and biting his own shield. The image is famous and it is, in part, true: it appears in poems and in sagas. But much of what is said today about berserkers is supposition, and the explanation that they ate mushrooms has no support in any early source.

## What the poems and sagas say

The oldest source is the poem *Haraldskvæði*, by Þorbjörn Hornklofi, composed around the year 900 for King Harald Fairhair. Describing the battle of Hafrsfjord, the poet says that the berserkers roared and the *úlfheðnar*, "wolf-skins", howled and shook iron spears.

Snorri Sturluson, in the *Ynglinga saga*, written around 1230, gives Odin men like this: they went to battle without mail, mad as dogs or wolves, bit their shields, were strong as bears or bulls and killed men without fire or iron harming them. Snorri gives this state the name *berserksgangr*, "going berserk".

## Bearskin or shirtless?

The origin of the word is debated. One reading is "bearskin", from *ber*, bear, and *serkr*, shirt. Another is "bare-shirt", that is, without armour. Both have defenders, and the sources do not settle the matter. The *úlfheðnar*, in turn, would be warriors in wolf-skins. Bronze plates found at Torslunda, on the Swedish island of Öland, from before the Viking Age, show a man with the skin and head of a wolf, and some researchers connect them to these warriors.

## Heroes or thugs

In the Icelandic sagas, written much later, berserkers are often not heroes. They appear as bullies who arrive at a farm and challenge the owner to a duel, to take his daughter or his goods, as in *Egils saga*. A saga hero generally ends up killing them.

The law also treated them as a problem. The *Grágás*, the collection of laws of medieval Iceland, provides a penalty of lesser outlawry for anyone who went *berserksgangr*. By this record, the state was seen as dangerous rather than as an honour.

## The mushroom hypothesis

The idea that berserkers ate the mushroom *Amanita muscaria* to enter their fury was proposed in 1784 by the Swede Samuel Ödmann. No old text speaks of mushrooms, and the effects of this fungus do not resemble what the sources describe. Other proposed explanations include rituals, drinks, the collective hysteria of a group in combat, and health disorders. None has been proved.

Historians also do not know whether berserkers existed as an organised group, as the sources suggest, or whether they are in part a literary theme, used to lend fear and fascination to battles.
