White-Bear-King-Valemon, in Norwegian Kvitebjørn kong Valemon, is a Norwegian fairy tale published as no. 90 in Norske Folke-Eventyr, Ny Samling, by Asbjørnsen and Moe, in 1871. George Webbe Dasent translated it in Tales from the Fjeld. The best-known version was collected by the artist August Schneider in 1870, in Setesdal. Moe also noted a variant from Bygland, summarized in the second edition, of 1852. This piece follows the article on the tale.

## The plot

A king has two ugly, mean daughters and a youngest who is beautiful and gentle. The youngest dreams of a golden wreath, and the goldsmiths get it wrong. She then sees, in the forest, a white bear wearing the wreath. The bear will give it only if she goes away with him, and she gets three days to prepare. The king tries to keep the beast away, but the bear defeats the army without a scratch.

The bear returns on three Thursdays in a row. The oldest and the middle daughter fail: when he asks whether they have ever sat softer or seen farther, they answer with their mother's lap and their father's court, and are sent back. The youngest says no, and he takes her to his castle. At night he becomes a man and lies down with her. Every year she has a child, and the bear takes it away right after the birth.

## The candle and the search

After three years, she asks to visit her parents, and her mother gives her a candle. She lights it to see her husband, and a drop of tallow wakes him. He reveals that, with one more month of patience, he would have been free of the spell of a witch queen. Now he will have to marry her. He leaves, and the girl clings to his fur until she falls, exhausted.

She searches through the forest and passes a series of huts. The first girl gives her scissors that make silk and velvet, the second a flask that pours whatever is wished for, and the third a cloth that gives food. At the fourth hut she feeds and clothes a poor family, and the smith makes iron claws for her to climb the mountain.

## The witch's castle

At the castle, she trades the objects for nights with her beloved. The witch drugs him, and he sleeps through the first two nights. On the third, a neighbouring craftsman who had overheard warns the king, and as he has not drunk the potion, the two can talk and make a plan. On the wedding day, the king has carpenters set up a hidden trapdoor on a bridge over a chasm. The witch and her bridesmaids fall. The spell is undone, the couple takes the treasures of the castle and goes to his homeland. The princess learns that the girls from the huts were her daughters, whom the king had taken to help her.

## The variants

The article cites variants from Åseral, Saltdal, Sweden, Swedish Finland and Germany. In Saltdal, the bear is a king's son and must marry a troll-woman who lives east of the Sun and west of the Moon, and the final test is washing wax from his shirt. The tale is of type ATU 425A, "The Animal as Bridegroom", and resembles East of the Sun and West of the Moon, also by Asbjørnsen and Moe.

## In short

It is a tale of an enchanted bridegroom, with a broken prohibition and a long search. The heroine wins with patience, with gifts and with the help of simple people.

## Terms

*ATU* is the index of tale types of Aarne, Thompson and Uther. *Tallow* is the fat of the candle. *Trapdoor* is a door in the floor.

## What remains uncertain

The versions differ in details, such as the final test, the witch and the place where she lives. The article does not say which is the oldest. The origin of the tale, before it was collected, is not known.
