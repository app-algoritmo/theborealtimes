Edmund was king of East Anglia, in eastern England, from about 855 until 20 November 869 (some calculations say 870). His story reaches us in two layers: a dry record and another, much later one, full of miracles.

## The army that arrived

The Great Heathen Army landed in East Anglia in the autumn of 865, probably with more than five thousand men. In 866 it moved on to York, in 867 it attacked Mercia and in 868 it returned. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, in its entry for 870, says Edmund fought them, lost and was killed.

## Abbo's passion

Around 986, the monk Abbo of Fleury wrote the *Passio*. He heard it from Dunstan, archbishop of Canterbury, who in turn heard it from an old man who claimed to have been Edmund's sword-bearer. In it the Viking leader is "Hinguar", probably Ivar the Boneless. Edmund refuses to renounce his faith and is bound to a tree. Ælfric's paraphrase speaks of arrows and beheading, and the story goes on with the head carried into the woods.

## The head and the wolf

Edmund's men searched for the head, calling to one another, and the head answered: *"Her, her, her"*, meaning "here, here, here". They found it among the trees, guarded by a great wolf, which let it go unharmed. The place of death is called Haegelisdun, perhaps Hoxne, where a stone cross marks the oak that fell in 1848, in whose trunk an arrowhead was found.

## Relics and coins

The relics went to Beodricesworth, today Bury St Edmunds, where a monastery was founded in 925. In 1010 they were taken to London for three years. The shrine was destroyed in 1539. Memorial coins with the legend *SCE EADMVND REX* were struck by more than 70 moneyers, and the Cuerdale Hoard, found in 1840, held more than 1,800 of them. Minting ended around 910. Feast day: 20 November. Edmund was, with Edward the Confessor, patron saint of England until Saint George took his place in the 15th century.
