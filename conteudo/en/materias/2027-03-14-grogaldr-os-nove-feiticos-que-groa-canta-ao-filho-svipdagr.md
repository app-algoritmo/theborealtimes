Among the poems of the Poetic Edda, few have a scene as strange as Grógaldr: a son goes to his mother's grave and wakes her to ask for help. The mother is Gróa, a seeress, and what she answers is a list of nine spells for a journey nobody wanted to make.

## The request

Svipdagr, the young man, calls his dead mother, who had told him to do so if he ever needed her. Gróa, a *völva*, or seeress, rises from the grave and asks why he woke her. He explains that his stepmother has set him a task: to win Menglöð, a woman whose hall lies in a place where, the stepmother says, "one cannot travel". Gróa admits the road is long and hard, but does not try to talk him out of it. He asks for spells of protection, and she sings.

## The nine spells

In Lee Hollander's translation, stanzas 6 to 14 hold nine "heeds". The first is a general charm against fear: what seems shocking must be shaken off, and the way will make itself. The second guards the weary traveller from mockery. The third calls on the rivers that run to the realm of the dead, so that the deep waters run dry before him. The fourth protects from foes who surround him to kill him: their hearts will hold back their hands. The fifth loosens bonds, if he is fettered hand and foot. The sixth calms the sea, if the weather is wilder than men know. The seventh guards against deadly cold on the fells. The eighth protects, on misty moors, from the wraith of a Christian woman, and Hollander notes that the original reads "Christian woman", a sign of the belief that such ghosts were even more dangerous to a heathen hero. The ninth puts words in the traveller's mouth when he contends with giants.

## The poem and the scholars

The text survives in several 17th-century paper manuscripts, together with Fjölsvinnsmál. For a long time nobody saw that they were parts of one story. In 1854, Svend Grundtvig linked Grógaldr to the first part of the medieval Scandinavian ballad *Ungen Sveidal*. In 1856, Sophus Bugge saw that the end of the ballad matches Fjölsvinnsmál, and in 1860 he called the pair Svipdagsmál, the name scholars adopted. Grógaldr is one of six Eddic poems that involve necromancy.

## In short

Svipdagr wakes his mother, Gróa, and she gives him nine spells of protection: against fear, mockery, waters, foes, fetters, a wild sea, cold, the dead and giants.

## Terms

A *völva* is a seeress who speaks with the dead and foresees the future. *Necromancy* is the practice of speaking with the dead. The *Poetic Edda* is the collection of mythological and heroic poems in Old Norse.

## What remains uncertain

The second spell is hard to translate, and Hollander admits doubt. The date of composition is debated. It is also debated whether the Gróa of this poem is the same one who treats Thor in the myth of Hrungnir, as some scholars propose.
