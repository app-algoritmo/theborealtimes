The Oddrúnargrátr, "lament of Oddrún", is one of the short poems of the Poetic Edda on the cycle of Sigurd and the Gjúkungs. It has no battles. It is a poem spoken by a woman, and almost all the action takes place in one room.

## The poem in the manuscript

According to the sources, the poem is anonymous, in fornyrðislag, and thought to be of relatively late composition, perhaps from the eleventh century. In the Codex Regius it comes after Guðrúnarkviða III and before Atlakviða. Bellows notes in his introduction that the base is the German legend of Sigurd and Atli, but Oddrún and the minor figures, Borgný and Vilmundr, are Northern additions.

## Borgný cannot give birth

Borgný, daughter of King Heiðrekr and wife of Vilmundr, is in labour and cannot give birth. Oddrún, Atli's sister, hears of her suffering, saddles a horse and rides to the hall. A serving-maid explains what is happening. Oddrún chants charms over Borgný, and a boy and a girl are born. Borgný thanks the gods, among them Frigg and Freyja.

## What Oddrún tells

Borgný calls her hard, and Oddrún answers by recalling the past. She then tells her story: she loved Gunnarr, "the breaker of rings". Atli refused the marriage gold the family of Gjúki offered and sent spies. They found the two lovers in the forest, accepted red rings to keep quiet and then told Atli everything.

The sons of Gjúki ride to Atli's hall. Högni has his heart cut out, and Gunnarr is thrown into the serpents' pit, where he plays the harp and hopes Oddrún will come. She hears the sad music and sails there, but arrives too late to save him.

## The last line

Oddrún ends by asking why she still lives, since she loved Gunnarr as herself. The last line, in Bellows's translation, is: "Each man lives as his longing wills", and then the poem ends, with the lament. According to Bellows's note, the poem implies that Atli's revenge on Gunnarr and Högni was partly prompted by his discovery of the affair between Gunnarr and his sister.

## In short

It is a poem about a woman who, after everything, still helps another woman give birth. It shows a side of the Sigurd cycle that the sagas of war do not show: the voice of someone who has lost a person and remains.
