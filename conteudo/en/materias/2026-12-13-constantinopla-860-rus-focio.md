In June 860, the inhabitants of Constantinople saw something that almost never happened: a foreign fleet, coming from the north, entering the Bosphorus strait and reaching the gates of the greatest city of the Christian world. They were the Rus, a people the sources link to Scandinavian merchants and warriors who operated on the rivers of present-day Russia and Ukraine. The attack is one of the first events of their history that can be dated.

## The undefended city

According to the *Brussels Chronicle*, which historians now accept as the best source for the date, the attack began on 18 June 860 and lasted until 4 August. Emperor Michael III was in Asia Minor, campaigning against the Abbasids, and the imperial army and navy were far away. The Rus attacked by surprise and plundered the suburbs and the Princes' Islands in the Sea of Marmara. Photios, the city's patriarch, described their arrival as "a thunderbolt from heaven".

## How many there were

The Greek sources speak of about two hundred ships. John the Deacon gives 360, and the *Primary Chronicle*, written in the 12th century, speaks of 2,000, a figure considered exaggerated. Scholars generally propose that the attackers numbered a few thousand men. The *Primary Chronicle* attributes the attack to two chiefs, Askold and Dir, but it also gets the date wrong, placing it in 866, probably through a chronological error.

## The legend of the robe

A popular story says that the emperor and the patriarch dipped the Virgin's robe, kept at Blachernae, into the sea, and that a storm scattered the Rus fleet. Orthodox tradition celebrates this episode. But historians regard it as a later legend, because Photios' August sermon does not mention it. Photios' homilies are the earliest sources and do not say how the attack ended or why the Rus withdrew. The later sources attribute the withdrawal to the emperor's quick return, but the earlier ones do not confirm this.

## A victory for whom?

The question of who won has no simple answer. In some lists "Rus victory" appears, but the account itself says the result is unknown in detail. The historian Constantine Zuckerman argues that the story of the Byzantine victory was invented later.

## What remains

The attack of 860 reveals how quickly the Byzantine world could be taken by surprise, and it marks the beginning of relations between Byzantium and the Rus, which would later lead to the conversion of Kievan Rus to Christianity. For scholars, it is also a clear example of how a pious legend can cover a more confusing fact. Photios' own homilies, preached to the people, show a shaken city that saw the attack as divine punishment and looked for explanations in faith.
