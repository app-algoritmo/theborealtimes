In parts of the east and north of England, many place-names end in -by or in -thorpe. This piece uses two sources, a text of the site viking.no and a page of the site family-tree.co.uk. They describe the most common endings and explain why caution is needed when drawing conclusions from a name.

## The Danelaw

The Danelaw is the region of England where, in the Viking Age, the Scandinavian presence was strong. According to the sources, there are there many hundreds of place-names of Scandinavian origin, and this is what allows one to speak of a Norse mark on the map.

## The most common endings

The ending *-by* means "farmstead" and is the most common, with a concentration in Lincolnshire. The ending *-thorpe* indicates a secondary settlement, which arises as an overflow of a larger one. Other endings cited are *-toft*, *-kirk* and *-ness*. The ending *-holme* is tied to land reclaimed from a marsh, and *-thwaite* is a clearing, and the sources say it would be "supposedly" more Norwegian. The endings *-wick* and *-wich*, and also *-borough*, are ambiguous, and may have other origins.

## Hybrid names

There are names formed from two languages. The source cites Grimston and Grimsby when speaking of hybrid names. The idea is that a name may mix elements of different origins, and so the ending alone does not always tell the origin of the whole name.

## Cautions

The sources warn that a Scandinavian name does not prove that whoever gave it was Scandinavian. Many names may have been created later. The spellings of the Domesday Book, the English record of the 11th century, vary, and this makes it hard to know the old form of a name.

## How the sources complete each other

The two sources agree on the essentials: *-by* is the most common ending and *-thorpe* indicates a secondary settlement. The message of caution comes from the sources themselves, which do not treat the name as proof. For that reason the map of endings is a starting point for questions, and not an answer about who lived in each place.

## In brief

The endings *-by* and *-thorpe* are the best known and are concentrated in the Danelaw. They indicate farmsteads and secondary settlements, and other endings complete the picture. What the map shows is a presence of Norse words, not a count of people.

## Terms

A *place-name* is the name of a place. A *settlement* is a small cluster of houses. *Hybrid* is what mixes two origins. The *Domesday Book* is an English record of the 11th century.

## What remains uncertain

Who the people were who gave the names is not said by the names. When each name was born, and whether it was in the time of the Vikings or after, is not always known. Some endings, like *-wick* and *-borough*, are ambiguous. The idea that *-thwaite* would be more Norwegian is only a supposition of the sources.
